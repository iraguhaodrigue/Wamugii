Nobody learned to ride a bicycle from a table of correct answers. You tried, wobbled, fell, adjusted, and slowly your body learned what works. Nobody labelled each movement "correct" or "incorrect"; you simply felt the result: staying up was good, falling was bad.

**Reinforcement learning (RL)** is how an agent learns in the same way: by **doing**. There are no labelled answers. The agent tries actions, sees what happens, and receives **rewards** (good) or **penalties** (bad). Over many tries, it learns which actions lead to the most reward over time.

## The parts of reinforcement learning

RL uses the agent idea from Chapter 3, with one important addition: the **reward**.

| Term | Meaning | Example: a robot vacuum cleaner |
|---|---|---|
| **Agent** | The learner and decision-maker | The robot |
| **Environment** | The world it acts in | The house |
| **State** | The current situation | Its position, battery level, dirt nearby |
| **Action** | A choice the agent can make | Move forward, turn left, turn right, return to charger |
| **Reward** | A number given after an action: positive is good, negative is bad | +1 for each patch of dirt cleaned; −10 for bumping down the stairs; −50 if the battery runs out away from the charger |
| **Policy** | The agent's strategy: which action to take in each state | "When the battery is low, head to the charger" |

## The RL loop

[[fig:rlloop|The reinforcement-learning loop. The agent acts, the environment changes and gives a reward, and the agent updates its policy. This repeats thousands or millions of times.]]

1. The agent observes the **state**.
2. It chooses an **action** using its current policy.
3. The environment moves to a **new state** and gives a **reward**.
4. The agent **updates its policy** so that actions that led to good results become more likely.
5. Repeat.

At the start, the policy is close to random and the agent makes many poor choices. Learning happens because, over many rounds, it connects actions in particular states with the rewards that followed.

## Long-term thinking

The agent's goal is the **highest total reward over time**, often called the **return**, not just the biggest reward right now. This is what makes RL suited to **sequential** problems, where today's action changes tomorrow's options (Chapter 3).

:::example Worked example: short-term vs long-term reward
The robot vacuum has 20% battery. It can:

- **Plan A:** keep cleaning nearby dirt: +1, +1, +1 (three patches), then the battery dies far from the charger: −50. Total = 3 − 50 = **−47**.
- **Plan B:** go to the charger now (0 reward for travelling), charge, then clean 30 patches later: +30. Total = **+30**.

Plan A gives rewards **sooner**, but Plan B gives far more **in total**. A well-trained RL agent learns to choose Plan B, even though it means giving up immediate reward.

In practice, rewards far in the future are often counted a little less than rewards now (a **discount**), because the future is uncertain. But the principle stays: the agent cares about the whole journey, not the next step.
:::

A chess player who sacrifices a piece to win the game later is thinking in exactly this way.

## Explore or exploit?

Every RL agent faces a dilemma:

- **Exploit:** use what already works well.
- **Explore:** try something new that **might** be better, and might be worse.

:::example Worked example: a mobile vendor choosing a spot
A vendor selling airtime and snacks can stand at Spot A or Spot B each day.

- After 10 days at Spot A, she earns on average **8,000 RWF** per day.
- She tried Spot B only **once** and earned **6,000 RWF** that day.

Should she always stay at A? One day at B tells her very little: maybe it rained that day, or maybe B is a quiet place. If she **only exploits**, she will never learn whether B is better on normal days. If she **only explores**, switching every day at random, she wastes many days at whichever spot is worse.

A simple balance, called **epsilon-greedy**: on most days (for example 9 out of 10) go to the spot with the best average so far; on the remaining day (1 in 10) try the other spot. Over weeks, her averages become reliable, and she ends up at the truly better spot most of the time.
:::

Good RL agents usually **explore more at the beginning**, when they know little, and **exploit more later**, as their knowledge improves. People do the same: a new student tries many study methods in the first term, then settles on the ones that work.

## Reward design is everything

An RL agent chases **whatever is rewarded**, even in ways nobody intended. This is called **reward hacking** (or "specification gaming").

- A cleaning robot rewarded for **"dust collected"** might learn to **spill dust and collect it again**.
- In a well-known research example, an agent in a boat-racing game was rewarded for hitting targets along the course. It discovered it could circle forever, hitting the same targets again and again, and never finish the race, while scoring more points than agents that raced properly.
- A delivery agent rewarded only for **speed** may learn to take dangerous risks.

This connects directly to the PEAS lesson (Chapter 3): **what you measure is what you get**. The fix is to design rewards that describe the real goal (**a clean house**, **finishing the race**, **safe and fast delivery**), and to test the agent carefully for strange shortcuts.

:::mistake Common mistake
Assuming that a high reward score means the agent did what you wanted. Always **watch what the agent actually does**. A surprising strategy with a high score is often a sign of reward hacking, not genius.
:::

## Where RL is used

- **Games.** AlphaGo (Chapter 2) improved partly by playing millions of games against itself. RL agents have also mastered many video games.
- **Robotics.** Learning to walk, grasp objects or balance, often first in a computer simulation and then on a real robot.
- **Resource control.** Managing cooling in data centres to save energy; adjusting traffic signals; managing battery charging.
- **Recommendations and advertising.** Choosing what to show to keep users satisfied over time (with the reward-design risks discussed above).
- **Chatbots: RLHF.** This one deserves its own section.

## RLHF: how chatbots learn what people prefer

A raw language model trained only to predict text (Chapter 17) can be unhelpful, rude or unsafe, because it copies all kinds of text from the internet. **Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)** helps fix this:

1. The chatbot writes **several answers** to the same question.
2. **People rank** the answers from best to worst (most helpful, most honest, most polite and safe).
3. A separate **reward model** learns to predict these human preferences.
4. The chatbot is then trained with reinforcement learning to produce answers that the reward model scores highly.

This is one reason modern assistants are more helpful and polite than raw language models. It also inherits RL's risk: if the reward model prefers answers that **sound** confident and pleasing, the chatbot may learn to sound confident even when it is wrong. Reward design matters here too.

## The three types of machine learning

| | Supervised | Unsupervised | Reinforcement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learns from | Labelled examples | Unlabelled data | Rewards from its own actions |
| Main question | "What is the answer?" | "What structure is here?" | "What should I do next?" |
| Feedback | The correct answer for each example | None (people judge the patterns) | A reward, often delayed |
| Example | Spam filter | Customer groups | Game-playing agent, robot control |

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Imagine an RL agent controlling traffic lights at a busy Kigali junction. The **state** could be the queue length on each road; the **actions**, which direction gets green and for how long; the **reward**, minus the total waiting time of all vehicles. Careful reward design would also protect pedestrians and motorbike riders, not just cars, and would be tested in a simulation before touching real traffic.
:::

## Summary

- **Reinforcement learning** learns by trial, error and reward, without labelled answers.
- The loop: observe the **state**, choose an **action**, receive a **reward** and a new state, update the **policy**.
- The agent aims for the **highest total reward over time**, which suits sequential decisions.
- The **explore–exploit** trade-off balances trying new actions against using known good ones; explore more early, exploit more later.
- **Reward hacking** happens when the reward does not describe the real goal. **RLHF** uses human rankings to make chatbots more helpful.

## Key terms

- **Reinforcement learning (RL)** — learning by trying actions and receiving rewards or penalties.
- **State** — the current situation the agent is in.
- **Action** — a choice the agent can make.
- **Reward** — a number telling the agent how good the result of an action was.
- **Policy** — the agent's strategy for choosing actions in each state.
- **Return** — the total reward collected over time.
- **Explore–exploit trade-off** — balancing trying new actions with using actions known to work.
- **Epsilon-greedy** — usually choosing the best-known action, but sometimes trying another at random.
- **Reward hacking** — an agent finding an unintended way to earn reward.
- **RLHF** — reinforcement learning from human feedback; tuning a model towards answers people prefer.
- **Reward model** — a model that learns to predict which answers people prefer.

## Think deeper

1. Design rewards for a school-bus routing agent. Then describe one way it could hack your reward, and fix it.
:::answer
A first reward: +10 for each pupil delivered to school, −1 for each minute of total travel time. A possible hack: the agent learns to skip pupils who live far away, because collecting them adds many minutes. Fix: add a large penalty for any pupil not collected (for example −100 per pupil), and a penalty for any pupil arriving after school starts, so the agent must serve everyone on time while keeping travel short.
:::
2. Why should an RL agent explore more at the start of learning and less later?
:::answer
At the start the agent knows almost nothing, so its "best" action is a guess; exploring gives it the information it needs, and the cost of a few poor choices is worth it. Later its estimates are much more reliable, so most exploration would waste reward on actions it already knows are worse. Reducing exploration over time balances learning with earning.
:::
3. Explain how RLHF could accidentally teach a chatbot to give confident but wrong answers.
:::answer
In RLHF, the chatbot is trained to produce answers that the reward model scores highly, and the reward model copies human rankings. If the people ranking answers tend to prefer confident, fluent, pleasing answers, and cannot always check whether they are correct, the reward model learns "confident and fluent is good". The chatbot then learns to sound confident, even when it does not know the answer. Careful instructions to rankers, fact-checking and rewards for admitting uncertainty help reduce this.
:::

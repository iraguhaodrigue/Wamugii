A company proudly announces: "Our fraud model is 95% accurate!" It sounds excellent. But suppose only 5% of transactions are fraud. Then a lazy "model" that says **"not fraud"** to every single transaction is also 95% accurate, and it catches **zero** fraud.

This chapter gives you the tools to see through numbers like that. By the end, you will be able to read a model's results like a professional and ask the questions that matter.

## Why accuracy is not enough

**Accuracy** is the share of all predictions that are correct. It is easy to understand, and useful when classes are **balanced** (for example, roughly half spam and half normal). But when one class is **rare** (fraud, disease, dropouts, faulty machines), accuracy is dominated by the common class and can hide complete failure on the rare one, which is usually the class we care about most.

## The confusion matrix

A **confusion matrix** compares the model's predictions with the truth in a 2 × 2 table. Take a mobile-money company with **1,000** transactions, of which **50** are really fraud. A model flags **60** transactions as fraud.

[[fig:confusion|The confusion matrix for the fraud model. Green cells are correct predictions; red cells are the two kinds of mistake.]]

| | **Really fraud** | **Really normal** |
|---|---|---|
| **Predicted fraud** | True positives (TP) = **40** | False positives (FP) = **20** |
| **Predicted normal** | False negatives (FN) = **10** | True negatives (TN) = **930** |

The words come from medical testing, where "positive" means "the test says yes, the thing is present":

- **True positive (TP):** fraud correctly caught.
- **False positive (FP):** a normal transaction wrongly flagged. A **false alarm**: an annoyed customer, a delayed payment.
- **False negative (FN):** fraud the model missed. A **miss**: money lost.
- **True negative (TN):** a normal transaction correctly left alone.

Check the totals: TP + FN = 40 + 10 = 50 real fraud cases. TP + FP = 40 + 20 = 60 flagged. All four cells add up to 1,000.

:::tip How to fill a confusion matrix without mistakes
Start with the facts you know. (1) How many are **really positive**? That column must add up to it. (2) How many did the model **flag**? That row must add up to it. (3) How many of the flagged were **correct**? That is TP. Everything else follows by subtraction.
:::

## The key measures

| Measure | Formula | Our model | The plain question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | (TP + TN) ÷ total | (40 + 930) ÷ 1,000 = **97%** | How often is it right overall? |
| **Precision** | TP ÷ (TP + FP) | 40 ÷ 60 = **67%** | When it says "fraud", how often is it really fraud? |
| **Recall** | TP ÷ (TP + FN) | 40 ÷ 50 = **80%** | Of all the real fraud, how much did it catch? |
| **F1 score** | 2 × P × R ÷ (P + R) | 2 × 0.67 × 0.80 ÷ 1.47 ≈ **0.73** | One number that balances precision and recall |

Two memory aids:

- **Precision** is about the model's **alarms**: are they trustworthy? (Look along the **predicted-fraud row**.)
- **Recall** is about the **real cases**: how many were found? (Look down the **really-fraud column**.) Recall is also called **sensitivity** in medicine.

The **F1 score** is high only when **both** precision and recall are reasonably high. A model with precision 100% and recall 2% has F1 of only about 0.04, which correctly signals that it is nearly useless.

Now compare with the lazy model that never flags anything: TP = 0, so recall = 0 ÷ 50 = **0%**, even though its accuracy is 95%. Recall exposes the failure that accuracy hid.

## Precision or recall: which matters more?

It depends on which mistake is **more costly** in the real world.

- **Recall first** when **missing** a case is dangerous: detecting serious disease, fraud, a child at risk of dropping out, a gas leak. It is better to check some extra cases than to miss real ones.
- **Precision first** when **false alarms** are costly: blocking a customer's account, sending an important email to spam, accusing someone of cheating, sending police to an address.

:::example Worked example: the malaria test
A model checks **200** patients; **20** really have malaria. The model flags **30** patients, of whom **18** really have malaria.

Fill the matrix: TP = 18. FP = 30 − 18 = **12**. FN = 20 − 18 = **2**. TN = 200 − 18 − 12 − 2 = **168**.

- Accuracy = (18 + 168) ÷ 200 = 186 ÷ 200 = **93%**.
- Precision = 18 ÷ 30 = **60%**.
- Recall = 18 ÷ 20 = **90%**.

Which matters most? **Recall.** A missed malaria case (false negative) can be fatal, while a false alarm (false positive) leads to an extra confirmation test. A recall of 90% means 2 sick patients were still missed, so the clinic should ask whether a lower threshold could catch them, and always confirm with a laboratory test.
:::

## The threshold: tuning the balance

Many classifiers output a **probability** ("this transaction is 73% likely to be fraud"). The team then chooses a **threshold**: flag a case if its probability is above, say, 0.7.

- **Lower the threshold** → the model flags **more** cases → it catches more real fraud (**recall up**), but more of its alarms are false (**precision down**).
- **Raise the threshold** → it flags **fewer** cases → its alarms are more trustworthy (**precision up**), but it misses more fraud (**recall down**).

[[fig:tradeoff|For the same fraud model, moving the threshold trades precision against recall. Lower thresholds catch more fraud but raise more false alarms.]]

There is usually no setting that makes both perfect. Choosing the threshold is a **business decision**, not a technical one: it depends on the cost of each mistake and on how many alarms the team can actually handle.

:::mistake Common mistake
Reporting precision **or** recall alone. A model can reach 100% recall simply by flagging **everything**, and very high precision by flagging only the one most obvious case. Always report both (or F1), together with the confusion matrix.
:::

## Measuring regression models

For number predictions, there is no "right or wrong", only "how far off". Two common measures:

- **MAE (mean absolute error):** the average size of the errors. Easy to explain: "our forecast is off by 0.75 tonnes on average".
- **RMSE (root mean squared error):** square each error, average them, then take the square root. Because errors are squared, **big mistakes count much more**.

:::example Worked example: MAE and RMSE
A maize-yield model predicts for four farms (tonnes):

| Farm | Real | Predicted | Error | Absolute error | Squared error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.0 | 2.5 | +0.5 | 0.5 | 0.25 |
| 2 | 3.0 | 2.5 | −0.5 | 0.5 | 0.25 |
| 3 | 4.0 | 4.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.00 |
| 4 | 5.0 | 7.0 | +2.0 | 2.0 | 4.00 |

- MAE = (0.5 + 0.5 + 0 + 2.0) ÷ 4 = 3.0 ÷ 4 = **0.75 tonnes**.
- RMSE = √((0.25 + 0.25 + 0 + 4.00) ÷ 4) = √(4.5 ÷ 4) = √1.125 ≈ **1.06 tonnes**.

RMSE is bigger than MAE because the one large error on farm 4 counts heavily. If big mistakes are especially costly (booking far too few trucks), RMSE is the better guide.
:::

## Always compare with a baseline

A score means little on its own. Always compare it with:

- the **lazy model** (always predict the most common class, or the average number),
- a **simple rule** (for example "flag transfers above 500,000 RWF"),
- the **current human process**.

"97% accuracy" is weak if the lazy model already gets 95%. "MAE of 0.75 tonnes" is strong if the current guess is off by 2 tonnes, and weak if last year's yield already predicts within 0.5 tonnes.

:::rwanda In Rwanda
When a vendor offers your organisation an AI product, ask for the **confusion matrix on data like yours**, not just "accuracy". A fraud tool tested on another country's transactions, or a health tool tested in a different hospital, may perform very differently on Rwandan data. Ask for precision and recall, the threshold used, and the results for different groups (Chapter 19).
:::

## Summary

- **Accuracy** can hide total failure when a class is rare.
- The **confusion matrix** shows true positives, false positives (false alarms), false negatives (misses) and true negatives.
- **Precision** = TP ÷ (TP + FP): how trustworthy the alarms are. **Recall** = TP ÷ (TP + FN): how much of the real problem is caught. **F1** balances them.
- Choose by **which mistake costs more**. The **threshold** trades precision against recall, and choosing it is a business decision.
- For regression, use **MAE** (average error) and **RMSE** (punishes big errors). Always compare with a **baseline**.

## Key terms

- **Accuracy** — the share of all predictions that are correct.
- **Confusion matrix** — a table comparing predicted classes with true classes.
- **True positive (TP)** — a real positive case correctly predicted as positive.
- **False positive (FP)** — a negative case wrongly predicted as positive; a false alarm.
- **False negative (FN)** — a positive case wrongly predicted as negative; a miss.
- **True negative (TN)** — a negative case correctly predicted as negative.
- **Precision** — TP ÷ (TP + FP); how often a positive prediction is right.
- **Recall (sensitivity)** — TP ÷ (TP + FN); the share of real positives that are found.
- **F1 score** — a single number combining precision and recall.
- **Threshold** — the probability cut-off above which a case is predicted positive.
- **MAE** — mean absolute error; the average size of errors in number predictions.
- **RMSE** — root mean squared error; like MAE but punishes large errors more.

## Think deeper

1. A spam filter checks 500 emails, of which 100 are really spam. It marks 90 as spam, and 85 of those are really spam. Fill in the confusion matrix and calculate accuracy, precision and recall.
:::answer
TP = 85. FP = 90 − 85 = 5. FN = 100 − 85 = 15. TN = 500 − 85 − 5 − 15 = 395. Accuracy = (85 + 395) ÷ 500 = 480 ÷ 500 = 96%. Precision = 85 ÷ 90 ≈ 94%. Recall = 85 ÷ 100 = 85%. For spam, high precision is important, because a false positive can hide an important real email.
:::
2. A bank's fraud team can review only 50 flagged transactions per day. How should this affect the choice of threshold?
:::answer
The threshold must be high enough that the model flags about 50 transactions a day or fewer, otherwise flags will pile up unreviewed and lose their value. Within that limit, the team wants the 50 flags to contain as much real fraud as possible, so high precision matters. If the limit means recall is too low, the bank should consider adding staff, or automatically handling the most certain cases.
:::
3. Why might a team prefer RMSE over MAE when forecasting hospital bed demand?
:::answer
Because a large under-forecast is especially dangerous: patients without beds. RMSE squares the errors, so one big mistake raises it much more than several small ones. Using RMSE pushes the team towards models that avoid large misses, which matches the real cost of errors in this setting.
:::

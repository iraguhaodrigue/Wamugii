How did you learn to recognise a ripe avocado? Probably nobody gave you a rule book. Someone showed you avocados and said "this one is ripe, this one is not", many times, until you could judge new ones yourself. **Supervised learning** works exactly like this: the computer learns from many examples that come with the correct answer, then answers new examples alone.

Supervised learning is the most widely used kind of machine learning in business today. Spam filters, credit scoring, crop-disease detection, delivery-time estimates and demand forecasts are all supervised.

## Learning with a teacher

In supervised learning, every training example comes with its **label**, the correct answer (Chapter 7). The labels act like a teacher's answer key.

The learning process:

1. Show the model many examples of **features and labels**.
2. The model makes predictions and measures how wrong they are. This measure is called the **error** or **loss**.
3. The model **adjusts itself** (its internal numbers, called **parameters**) to reduce that error.
4. Repeat until the error stops falling.
5. Use the finished model on **new** examples that have no label. This is **inference** (Chapter 1).

Supervised learning handles both **classification** (category answers) and **regression** (number answers).

## Method 1: linear regression

**Linear regression** draws the **best straight line** through the data. "Best" means the line that makes the total error between the line and the real points as small as possible.

:::example Worked example: house prices
Suppose a model learns from past sales in one neighbourhood that:

> price (millions RWF) = 10 + 0.25 × area (m²)

- The **10** is the starting value (the price when area is zero, a mathematical starting point rather than a real house).
- The **0.25** is the **slope**: each extra square metre adds about 0.25 million RWF (250,000 RWF).

Prediction for a 120 m² house: 10 + 0.25 × 120 = 10 + 30 = **40 million RWF**.

With more features, the idea is the same: price = starting value + (amount per m²) × area + (amount per bedroom) × bedrooms + …
:::

- **Strengths:** simple, fast, and easy to explain ("each extra square metre adds 250,000 RWF").
- **Weaknesses:** only captures straight-line relationships. If prices rise faster for big houses, or depend on combinations of features, a straight line misses it.

## Method 2: logistic regression

Despite its name, **logistic regression** is used for **classification**. It works out a score from the features (like linear regression) and then squeezes that score into a **probability between 0 and 1**, for example "82% chance this transaction is fraud".

A business rule then turns the probability into a decision, using a **threshold**: "flag as fraud if the probability is above 0.7". Chapter 14 shows how choosing this threshold changes the balance between catching fraud and annoying honest customers.

- **Strengths:** gives probabilities, not just yes/no; easy to explain which features push the probability up or down. Widely used in banking and health for exactly these reasons.
- **Weaknesses:** like linear regression, it handles only fairly simple patterns.

## Method 3: decision trees and random forests

A **decision tree** asks a series of **yes/no questions**, like a flowchart, until it reaches an answer.

:::example Worked example: a loan decision tree
A tree learned from past loans might look like this:

- Question 1: **Monthly sales above 100,000 RWF?**
  - **No** → predict **will not repay** (most similar past members did not repay).
  - **Yes** → Question 2: **Fewer than 2 past late payments?**
    - **Yes** → predict **will repay**.
    - **No** → predict **will not repay**.

Test it on three new members:
- Member X: sales 150,000, late payments 0 → Q1 yes → Q2 yes → **repay**.
- Member Y: sales 70,000, late payments 0 → Q1 no → **not repay**.
- Member Z: sales 200,000, late payments 3 → Q1 yes → Q2 no → **not repay**.

The algorithm chose these questions and the 100,000 cut-off itself, by finding the questions that best separate repaid from unpaid loans in the training data.
:::

- **Strengths:** easy for people to read and check; handles both categories and numbers; captures combinations ("high sales **and** few late payments").
- **Weaknesses:** a single deep tree can **overfit**: it keeps adding questions until it memorises the training data (Chapter 13).

A **random forest** combines **many** decision trees, each trained on a slightly different random sample of the data and features. The trees **vote**, and the majority answer wins. Like asking many advisers instead of one, the forest is usually more accurate and more stable than any single tree, but it is harder to read.

## Method 4: k-nearest neighbours (k-NN)

**k-nearest neighbours** classifies a new example by looking at the **k most similar** past examples and taking a **vote**. In plain words: "members who look like you mostly repaid, so you probably will too".

:::example Worked example: k-NN with k = 3
Past members, with two features: years in the cooperative and past late payments.

| Member | Years | Late payments | Repaid? | Distance to new member X (3, 1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 0 | Yes | 2.24 |
| B | 1 | 3 | No | 2.83 |
| C | 9 | 1 | Yes | 6.00 |
| D | 2 | 2 | No | 1.41 |
| E | 4 | 1 | Yes | 1.00 |
| F | 0 | 2 | No | 3.16 |

Distance here is the straight-line distance. For example, for member D: the difference in years is 3 − 2 = 1 and in late payments 1 − 2 = −1, so distance = √(1² + 1²) = √2 ≈ 1.41.

The 3 nearest neighbours are **E** (1.00, Yes), **D** (1.41, No) and **A** (2.24, Yes). The vote is 2 Yes against 1 No, so k-NN predicts that member X **will repay**.
:::

- **Strengths:** very intuitive; no real training step (it just stores the examples).
- **Weaknesses:** slow when there are millions of examples, because it must measure distance to all of them; and features must be on **similar scales**. If one feature is sales in RWF (values like 150,000) and another is late payments (values like 2), the sales numbers will dominate the distance. Teams fix this by **scaling** features to similar ranges first.

## Comparing the four methods

| Method | Problem type | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear regression | Regression | Simple, explainable | Only straight-line patterns |
| Logistic regression | Classification | Gives probabilities, explainable | Simple patterns only |
| Decision tree / random forest | Both | Readable rules (tree); strong accuracy (forest) | One tree can overfit; a forest is harder to read |
| k-nearest neighbours | Both | Intuitive, no training step | Slow on large data; needs scaled features |

**Neural networks** (Week 3) are also supervised learners. They are the most powerful kind for images, sound and language, but they need much more data and computing, and are the hardest to explain.

:::key How professionals choose
Start with a **simple, explainable** model as a baseline (linear or logistic regression, or a small tree). Move to a random forest or a neural network only if it is **clearly better** on validation data **and** the extra complexity is acceptable for the decision. For decisions about people's money, health or rights, being able to explain the model is often as important as a small gain in accuracy.
:::

## Where the labels come from

Labels are often the **expensive** part of supervised learning. Someone must mark thousands of leaf photos as *healthy* or *diseased*, emails as *spam* or *not spam*, or transactions as *fraud* or *normal*. This work is called **annotation** or **labelling**, and it is done by people, including many data workers in Africa who label data for global technology companies.

Labels must be **accurate and consistent**:

- **Clear labelling rules** ("a leaf counts as diseased if more than 10% of its surface shows spots") reduce disagreement.
- **Several labellers** for the same example, plus a check where they disagree, catch mistakes.
- **Experts** are needed for hard labels, such as medical images.

A supervised model copies the patterns in its labels. If labels are wrong or biased, the model will be wrong or biased too (Chapter 19).

:::mistake Common mistake
Thinking that "more complex model" always means "better model". On small or simple tabular data, a well-prepared logistic regression or random forest often matches or beats a neural network, while being faster, cheaper and easier to explain.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Supervised learning is behind many systems you meet here: fraud scoring in mobile money, credit scoring for small loans, delivery-time estimates in ride-hailing and e-commerce apps, and crop-disease apps that learned from photos labelled by agronomists. In each case, someone first had to collect examples **with the right answers**.
:::

## Summary

- Supervised learning learns from examples **with correct answers (labels)**, adjusting its parameters to reduce error, then predicts labels for new examples.
- **Linear regression** fits a straight line for number predictions. **Logistic regression** gives class probabilities.
- **Decision trees** ask yes/no questions; **random forests** combine many trees by voting. **k-NN** votes among the most similar past examples.
- Start simple and explainable; move to complex models only when they clearly help.
- Labels are expensive, human work. Wrong or inconsistent labels teach the model mistakes.

## Key terms

- **Supervised learning** — learning from examples that include the correct answers.
- **Parameters** — the internal numbers a model adjusts while learning.
- **Loss (error)** — a number measuring how wrong a model's predictions are.
- **Linear regression** — a method that fits the best straight line to predict a number.
- **Logistic regression** — a classification method that outputs a probability between 0 and 1.
- **Threshold** — the cut-off that turns a probability into a decision.
- **Decision tree** — a model that predicts by asking a series of yes/no questions.
- **Random forest** — many decision trees whose votes are combined.
- **k-nearest neighbours (k-NN)** — predicting from a vote of the k most similar past examples.
- **Feature scaling** — putting features on similar ranges so none dominates by size alone.
- **Annotation (labelling)** — the human work of adding correct answers to examples.

## Think deeper

1. Using the house-price model (price = 10 + 0.25 × area), predict the price of an 80 m² house. Then name one reason the real price could be very different.
:::answer
Price = 10 + 0.25 × 80 = 10 + 20 = 30 million RWF. The real price could differ because the model only uses area. It ignores location, condition, number of rooms, access to roads and water, and market changes since the data was collected. A straight line based on one feature is a rough estimate, not the truth.
:::
2. In the k-NN example, what would the prediction be for member X with k = 1? With k = 5? What does this tell you about choosing k?
:::answer
With k = 1, the nearest neighbour is E (Yes), so the prediction is "repay". With k = 5, the five nearest are E (Yes), D (No), A (Yes), B (No) and F (No): 2 Yes and 3 No, so the prediction becomes "not repay". The answer depends on k. A very small k is sensitive to one unusual neighbour; a large k may include examples that are not really similar. Teams test several values of k on validation data.
:::
3. A hospital must choose between a random forest with 91% accuracy and a logistic regression with 89% accuracy for predicting which patients need follow-up calls. What questions would you ask before choosing?
:::answer
Ask whether the 2-point difference is reliable on new data or just noise; how each model performs on the measure that matters most (likely recall, so high-risk patients are not missed) and for each patient group; whether doctors need to understand and explain the decisions; and how hard each model is to maintain. If the gain is small and explanation matters, the logistic regression may be the better choice.
:::

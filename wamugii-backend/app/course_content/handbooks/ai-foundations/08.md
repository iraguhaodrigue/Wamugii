A cooperative in Eastern Province asks: "Can AI tell us how much maize our farmers will harvest, so we can book enough storage and trucks?" A beginner jumps straight to "which model should we use?". A professional first asks: *who will use the forecast, how accurate must it be, what data exists, and how will the cooperative actually receive the numbers?*

Real machine-learning projects follow a repeatable path, called the **ML workflow**. Knowing it helps you plan, estimate time and cost, and talk clearly with engineers, managers and clients. It also explains why so many AI projects fail: not because the model was bad, but because a stage was skipped.

## The seven stages

[[fig:workflow|The machine-learning workflow. Monitoring sends the team back to earlier stages, so the workflow is a loop.]]

| # | Stage | Key question | Maize-yield example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Define the problem** | What decision will the prediction support, and how do we measure success? | Plan storage and transport; success = forecast within 10% of the real harvest |
| 2 | **Collect data** | What data exists, who owns it, and may we use it? | Past yields per farm, rainfall, soil type, seed variety, fertiliser use |
| 3 | **Prepare the data** | Is it clean, complete, representative and free of leakage? | Fix missing rainfall, standardise district names, remove duplicate farms |
| 4 | **Choose and train a model** | Which method fits the problem type? | A regression model, because yield is a number |
| 5 | **Evaluate** | How well does it work on data it has never seen? | Test on last season's farms, kept aside from training |
| 6 | **Deploy** | How will people actually use it? | A simple dashboard, or an SMS to the cooperative manager each month |
| 7 | **Monitor and improve** | Is it still accurate as the world changes? | Compare forecasts with real harvests each season; retrain with new data |

## Stage 1: define the problem (the most important stage)

Many failed projects had good models but a **badly defined problem**. Good problem definition answers:

- **Who** will use the prediction, and **what will they do differently** because of it? If nobody changes a decision, the model has no value.
- What does **"good enough"** mean, **in numbers**? "Accurate" is not a target; "within 10% of the real harvest for 8 out of 10 farms" is.
- What is the **problem type** (Chapter 4)?
- What would a **simple non-AI solution** achieve? Sometimes last year's average plus a spreadsheet rule is enough, and AI adds cost without adding value.
- What are the **costs of mistakes**? Over-forecasting means paying for empty trucks; under-forecasting means maize left to rot.

:::key Key idea
A model is a tool for a **decision**. Define the decision and the success measure first; choose the model last.
:::

## Stage 2: collect data

Ask where the data comes from, who owns it, and whether you may use it for this purpose (Chapter 7). Data collection often takes much longer than expected: records are on paper, in different offices, or in different formats. Plan for it.

## Stage 3: prepare the data

This stage often takes **the most time**. It includes cleaning (missing values, errors, duplicates, inconsistent formats), checking that the data is **representative**, removing **leakage**, and turning raw data into useful features.

### Feature engineering

**Feature engineering** means creating better inputs from raw data, often using knowledge of the field:

- From a **date**, create *month*, *season* (rainy or dry) and *day of the week*.
- From **total sales** and **number of days**, create *average daily sales*.
- From **rainfall per day**, create *total rainfall in the planting month*.
- From **GPS coordinates**, create *distance to the nearest market*.

A farmer or agronomist may know that rainfall in the **first month after planting** matters most. Turning that knowledge into a feature can improve a simple model more than switching to a complicated one. This is one reason **domain experts** belong in the team.

## Stage 4: choose and train a model

Choose a method that fits the problem type and the data (Chapter 9 describes common methods). Professionals start with a **baseline**: the simplest reasonable approach, such as "predict last year's yield" or a simple linear regression. The baseline gives a reference point. A complex model must clearly **beat** the baseline to be worth its extra cost, time and difficulty to explain.

## Stage 5: evaluate

A model must be tested on examples it did **not** learn from. Otherwise you only measure its memory, not its skill, like a student tested on the exact questions they practised. Chapter 13 explains how to split data into training, validation and test sets, and Chapter 14 explains how to measure performance.

Evaluation should also check performance for **different groups**, for example small and large farms, or different districts (Chapter 19). A model that is good on average may be poor for some groups.

## Stage 6: deploy

**Deployment** means putting the model into real use. The key question is: **how will people actually receive and use the result?**

- A cooperative manager with a basic phone needs an **SMS**, not a complicated web dashboard.
- A bank needs the fraud score inside its **payment system** within milliseconds.
- A clinic needs a result that fits into the nurse's existing routine, not one more app to open.

Many good models fail at this stage because the output does not fit the user's day.

## Stage 7: monitor and improve

The world changes: prices rise, rainfall patterns shift, customers change habits, fraudsters invent new tricks. A model trained on old data slowly becomes less accurate. This is called **model drift**.

- **Data drift:** the inputs change. A new seed variety spreads, so farms look different from the training data.
- **Concept drift:** the relationship between inputs and the answer changes. A new pest means the same rainfall now produces lower yields.

Monitoring compares predictions with what really happened, and watches for changes in the input data. When performance drops, the team goes back to stages 2–5: collect new data, prepare it, retrain and evaluate. That is why the workflow is a **loop**.

:::example Worked example: a full plan in brief
**Problem:** a pharmacy chain in Kigali often runs out of popular medicines.

1. **Define:** predict next week's sales of each of the top 100 medicines at each branch, so managers can order enough. Success: forecast within 15% for at least 80% of items. Baseline: "same as last week".
2. **Collect:** two years of daily sales per branch, prices, public holidays, school terms, rainy seasons.
3. **Prepare:** remove days when the system was down; standardise medicine names; engineer features such as *week of the year*, *holiday this week*, *sales in the same week last year*.
4. **Train:** start with the baseline, then a regression model.
5. **Evaluate:** train on the first 20 months, test on the last 4 months; check results per branch.
6. **Deploy:** an order suggestion in the system managers already use every Monday.
7. **Monitor:** compare forecasts with real sales weekly; retrain monthly; alert if errors grow (for example during a disease outbreak).
:::

:::mistake Common mistakes
- Starting with "we want to use deep learning" instead of "we want to make this decision better".
- Skipping the baseline, so nobody knows if the model is worth its cost.
- Testing on the same data used for training.
- Building a dashboard nobody opens; forgetting how the user really works.
- Treating deployment as the end and never checking for drift.
:::

## Who does what

| Role | Main contribution |
|---|---|
| **Domain experts** (farmers, doctors, accountants, teachers) | Define the problem, suggest features, judge whether results make sense |
| **Data engineers** | Collect, store and move data reliably |
| **Data scientists / ML engineers** | Prepare data, train and evaluate models, and put them into production |
| **Software engineers** | Build the app or system people use |
| **Product managers** | Keep everyone focused on the user's real need and the decision being improved |

In a small organisation, one person may play several roles. What matters is that every role's questions are asked.

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Many promising AI pilots stop at stage 5: the model works in a test, but nobody planned stages 6 and 7. Who pays for the server? Who checks the model each season? What happens when the internet is down? Planning deployment and monitoring from day one is what turns a pilot into a service people can rely on.
:::

## Summary

- The seven stages: **define → collect → prepare → train → evaluate → deploy → monitor**, then loop back.
- **Defining the problem** is the most important stage: the decision, the users, a numeric success measure and a simple alternative.
- **Feature engineering** creates better inputs from raw data using field knowledge. A **baseline** is the simple approach every model must beat.
- Deployment must fit how people actually work. **Model drift** makes models worse over time, so monitoring is essential.

## Key terms

- **ML workflow** — the repeatable stages of a machine-learning project.
- **Feature engineering** — creating more useful features from raw data.
- **Baseline** — the simplest reasonable method, used as a reference point.
- **Deployment** — putting a model into real use.
- **Model drift** — a model becoming less accurate because the world has changed.
- **Data drift** — a change in the inputs a model receives.
- **Concept drift** — a change in the relationship between the inputs and the answer.
- **Domain expert** — a person with deep knowledge of the field the model serves.

## Think deeper

1. A team spent three months building a very accurate model, but the client never used it. Name two stages that were probably done badly, and why.
:::answer
Stage 1 was probably done badly: the team may not have understood which decision the client needed to make or what output would help them. Stage 6 was probably also weak: the result may not have fitted the client's daily work, for example a complex dashboard for someone who only uses a basic phone. Accuracy alone does not create value if nobody can or will use the result.
:::
2. Explain the difference between data drift and concept drift, using a mobile-money fraud example.
:::answer
Data drift: the inputs change, for example many new customers join from a new region, so transaction patterns look different from the training data. Concept drift: the link between inputs and the answer changes, for example fraudsters invent a new trick, so transactions that used to be safe now are often fraud. In both cases the model's accuracy can fall, and monitoring is needed to notice.
:::
3. Why should a team build a simple baseline before a complex model?
:::answer
The baseline shows what can be achieved cheaply. If the complex model is only slightly better, its extra cost, time and difficulty to explain may not be worth it. If the complex model is much better, the baseline proves it. Without a baseline, a score such as "85%" has no meaning, because nobody knows whether a simple rule already reaches 84%.
:::

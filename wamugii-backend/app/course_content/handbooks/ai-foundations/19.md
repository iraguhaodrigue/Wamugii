A model does not need to be told to discriminate in order to discriminate. It learns patterns from data, and data is a record of the past. If the past was unfair, a model can quietly repeat that unfairness, and because computers seem objective, people may trust the unfair result even more.

This chapter explains how bias gets into AI systems, how to detect it with numbers, and what professionals do about it. It is one of the most important chapters in this handbook, because these systems make decisions about people's loans, jobs, health and education.

## When is a model unfair?

In this chapter, **bias** means something different from the statistical bias in Chapter 13. An AI system is **biased in the fairness sense** when it gives **systematically worse results for some groups of people**, for example by gender, age, region, language, skin colour, disability or income.

"Systematically" is the key word. Every model makes mistakes. The problem is when the mistakes **fall more heavily on one group**, again and again.

## Real cases

- **Face analysis.** The *Gender Shades* study (2018) by **Joy Buolamwini** and **Timnit Gebru** tested commercial systems that classify gender from face photos. For lighter-skinned men, error rates were **under 1%**. For darker-skinned women, error rates reached about **35%** in the worst system. The training images had contained far fewer darker-skinned faces, especially women's. After the study was published, several companies improved their systems.
- **Hiring.** In 2018 it was reported that **Amazon** had stopped using an experimental CV-screening tool after finding that it rated CVs lower when they included the word "women's" (as in "women's football club captain"). It had learned from ten years of past hiring decisions, which were mostly of men.
- **Health.** A widely used US health-care algorithm (studied in 2019) used past **health spending** as a stand-in for **health need**. Because less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same level of illness, the algorithm underestimated their needs. Fixing the measure greatly increased the number of Black patients identified for extra care.
- **Language.** Many AI tools work well in English and poorly in Kinyarwanda, Kirundi or Luganda, so speakers of those languages receive worse service from the same product.

## Where bias comes from

| Source | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical bias** | The data accurately records an unfair past | Past loans were given mostly to men, so "successful borrower" looks male |
| **Representation bias** | Some groups are missing or rare in the data | Few rural farmers, older people or darker-skinned faces in the training set |
| **Measurement bias** | A feature measures the real thing differently for different groups | Using "number of arrests" as a stand-in for "crime", or "health spending" for "health need" |
| **Labelling bias** | The people labelling bring their own assumptions | Annotators judging which photos look "professional" |
| **Deployment bias** | A model is used where it was not designed or tested | A model built on Kigali data used unchanged in a rural district |

Bias can enter at **every stage** of the ML workflow (Chapter 8): the problem definition, data collection, labelling, feature choice, evaluation and deployment.

### Feedback loops

Bias can also **grow over time**. Suppose a loan model rarely approves people from one area. Those people get fewer loans, so the lender collects little repayment data about them, so the next model knows even less about them and approves them even less. The model's own decisions shape its future training data.

## Proxy features: why deleting a column is not enough

A natural idea is: "If we remove the gender column, the model cannot be biased against women." Unfortunately, this is **not enough**.

Other features can act as **proxies**: they carry the same information indirectly.

- A **first name** often reveals gender.
- A **home district or village** can reveal ethnicity, income level or language.
- **School attended** can reveal income and region.
- **Phone model** or **mobile-money activity** can reveal income.
- **Career gaps** can reveal gender, because women more often take time off for childcare.

A model trained to copy biased past decisions will find these proxies and use them. Worse, once the sensitive column is deleted, it becomes **harder to check** whether the model is fair, because you can no longer compare groups. Many teams therefore keep sensitive attributes **for checking fairness**, even if they are **not used as model inputs**, with strong privacy protections.

:::mistake Common mistake
Believing that a model is fair because it "does not use gender, ethnicity or religion". Fairness must be **measured**, group by group. It cannot be assumed from the list of inputs.
:::

## Measuring fairness

Check results **separately for each group**, not only overall.

:::example Worked example: an average that hides harm
A model is tested on 1,000 people: 800 urban and 200 rural.

- Urban accuracy: 96% → 768 correct out of 800.
- Rural accuracy: 75% → 150 correct out of 200.
- Overall: (768 + 150) ÷ 1,000 = **91.8%**, reported as "92% accurate".

The overall number looks excellent, but rural users get wrong answers **one time in four**, six times as often as urban users (25% error against 4%). The average hides the harm, because rural people are a small share of the test data.
:::

Useful per-group checks:

- **Accuracy** for each group.
- **Recall** for each group: does the model catch high-risk patients, fraud or at-risk pupils equally well everywhere? (Missing more cases in one group means that group receives less help.)
- **False-positive rate** for each group: are innocent people in one group flagged more often?
- **Approval or selection rate** for each group.

:::example Worked example: comparing approval rates
A loan model approves **60%** of male applicants and **42%** of female applicants.

Approval-rate ratio = 42% ÷ 60% = **0.70**.

A common rule of thumb from US employment practice, the **four-fifths rule**, treats a ratio **below 0.8** as a warning sign worth investigating. A ratio of 0.70 does not prove discrimination by itself (there might be real differences in the applications), but it means the team must look carefully at **why** the rates differ and whether proxies or historical bias are involved.
:::

### Fairness has more than one definition

Researchers have proposed several definitions of fairness, for example "equal approval rates" or "equal recall for people who would really repay". When groups differ in their real outcome rates, it is mathematically **impossible** to satisfy all definitions at once with an imperfect model. Choosing which fairness goal matters most for a particular decision is therefore a **values decision**, made together with the people affected, managers and, where relevant, regulators, not a purely technical one.

## Practical steps to reduce bias

1. **Ask who could be harmed** at the very start of the project, not after launch.
2. **Collect representative data:** include the groups the model will serve, in sufficient numbers.
3. **Check the labels and measures:** is the label really what you care about (health **need**, not health **spending**)?
4. **Look for proxies** and test how well other features predict sensitive attributes.
5. **Measure performance per group** before deployment, and **keep measuring** after.
6. **Involve the affected community** and domain experts in design and testing.
7. **Keep a human in the loop** for high-stakes decisions such as loans, jobs, health, education and justice.
8. **Document** the data, its limits and the fairness checks in a **model card**: a short document describing what the model is for, how it was tested, and where it works less well.

:::key Key idea
Fairness is not a feature you switch on. It is a **practice**: asking who could be harmed, measuring per group, fixing what you find, and checking again as the world changes.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
A credit-scoring model trained mostly on data from salaried city workers may unfairly score farmers, informal traders and people with irregular income, who may repay well but look different on paper. Mobile-money activity can be a useful signal, but it can also act as a proxy for location and income. Measuring approval and repayment-prediction accuracy separately for rural and urban applicants, women and men, and formal and informal workers is the minimum responsible step.
:::

## Summary

- An AI system is **biased** (fairness sense) when it gives **systematically worse results for some groups**. It can happen without anyone intending it.
- Real cases include face analysis, hiring tools, health algorithms and languages.
- Bias sources: **historical, representation, measurement, labelling** and **deployment**. **Feedback loops** can make bias grow.
- Removing a sensitive column is not enough: **proxy features** carry the same information.
- **Measure per group**: accuracy, recall, false-positive rate and approval rate. A ratio below **0.8** is a warning sign. Fairness definitions can conflict; choosing one is a values decision.
- Reduce bias with representative data, better labels, per-group checks, community involvement, human oversight and documentation.

## Key terms

- **Bias (fairness)** — systematically worse results for some groups of people.
- **Historical bias** — bias from data that accurately records an unfair past.
- **Representation bias** — bias from groups being missing or rare in the data.
- **Measurement bias** — bias from a feature that measures the real thing differently for different groups.
- **Labelling bias** — bias introduced by the assumptions of the people labelling data.
- **Deployment bias** — bias from using a model in a setting it was not built or tested for.
- **Feedback loop** — when a model's decisions shape its future training data, which can make bias grow.
- **Proxy feature** — a feature that indirectly carries sensitive information, such as a name revealing gender.
- **Approval-rate ratio** — one group's approval rate divided by another's; below 0.8 is a common warning sign.
- **Four-fifths rule** — a rule of thumb treating an approval-rate ratio below 0.8 as possible unfair impact.
- **Model card** — a short document describing a model's purpose, testing, performance by group and limits.
- **Human in the loop** — a person reviewing or making the final decision that a model supports.

## Think deeper

1. A hiring model approves 30% of applicants from one university and 12% from another. Calculate the ratio and explain what the team should do next.
:::answer
Ratio = 12% ÷ 30% = 0.40, far below the 0.8 warning level. The team should investigate why: is the model using features that act as proxies for the university or for students' backgrounds? Were past hiring decisions (the labels) biased towards one university? Do the two groups really differ in job-relevant skills? They should test the model's accuracy for each group, consider removing or changing biased features or labels, and keep a person reviewing decisions until the cause is understood.
:::
2. Explain, with an example, how a feedback loop could make a model for police patrols more biased over time.
:::answer
Suppose a model sends more patrols to an area where more crimes were recorded in the past. More patrols in that area means more crimes are noticed and recorded there, while crimes elsewhere go unrecorded. The next model, trained on these new records, sees even more crime in the same area and sends even more patrols. The area appears more and more dangerous because of where the model looked, not necessarily because more crime happens there.
:::
3. Why might a team keep gender data for checking fairness, even though the model does not use it as an input?
:::answer
Without gender data, the team cannot measure whether the model treats women and men differently, and proxies could still create unfair results unnoticed. Keeping the attribute separately, securely and with a clear lawful purpose lets the team compare accuracy, recall and approval rates by group. It must be protected carefully and used only for checking, in line with data-protection law.
:::

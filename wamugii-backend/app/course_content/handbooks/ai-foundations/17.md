Type a question into ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude and, within seconds, you get a fluent answer that seems to understand you. It can write an email, explain photosynthesis, translate a letter or fix a bug in code. How does it work?

The answer is surprising. At its core, a **large language model (LLM)** does **one** basic job, again and again: **given some text, predict what comes next**. This chapter explains how that simple job, done at enormous scale with a clever design called the **transformer**, produces such capable systems, and why they still make confident mistakes.

## A machine that predicts the next word

You already do next-word prediction. Complete this sentence: *"Kigali is the capital of…"* You thought **Rwanda**. Complete *"Murakoze…"* and many Rwandans think **cyane**.

An LLM does this with a probability for every possible next piece of text. It picks one, adds it to the text, and predicts again. Repeat a few hundred times and you have a paragraph.

This sounds too simple to be intelligent. But to predict well across billions of sentences from books, websites, code and conversations, the model must learn **grammar**, **facts**, **styles of writing** and **patterns of reasoning**. Predicting the next word of a chemistry textbook well requires knowing some chemistry; predicting the next line of code requires knowing how code works.

## Step 1: tokens

Computers work with numbers, so text is first cut into **tokens**. A token can be a whole word, part of a word, or a punctuation mark. For example, a common English word like "the" may be one token, while a long or rare word may be split into several pieces. Each token has a number ID in the model's vocabulary.

Why does this matter?

- Models have limits measured **in tokens**, not words (the context window, below).
- Using an AI service is often **priced per token**.
- **Languages with less training data**, including Kinyarwanda, are often cut into **more, smaller tokens** for the same meaning, because the tokeniser was built mostly from other languages' text. That makes the model slower and more expensive for them, and often less accurate.

## Step 2: embeddings

Each token is turned into an **embedding**: a long list of numbers (often hundreds or thousands) that captures its **meaning**. These numbers are learned during training. Words used in similar ways end up with similar numbers.

:::example Worked example: a tiny embedding
Real embeddings have hundreds of numbers. Imagine only two, which we can read as "about people in education" and "about food":

| Word | Education | Food |
|---|---|---|
| teacher | 0.9 | 0.1 |
| lecturer | 0.8 | 0.1 |
| banana | 0.0 | 0.9 |
| canteen | 0.5 | 0.7 |

"Teacher" and "lecturer" have very similar numbers, so the model treats them as close in meaning. "Teacher" and "banana" are far apart. "Canteen" sits in between: it is connected to schools **and** food. Real embeddings capture thousands of such relationships at once, and nobody chooses the dimensions by hand: they emerge from training.
:::

## Step 3: attention, the key idea

The word **bank** means different things in *"the bank gave me a loan"* and *"we sat on the river bank"*. To understand a word, you must look at the **other words around it**.

**Attention** lets every token look at every other token in the text and decide **which ones matter most** for its meaning. Each token gets a new, context-aware version of its embedding, mixing in information from the tokens it attends to.

[[fig:attention|When the model processes "it", attention weighs the other words. Most weight goes to "cow", so the model links "it" to the cow. Numbers are illustrative.]]

In *"The farmer sold the cow because it was old"*, attention helps the model link **it** to **cow**: old cows are sold; the sentence makes less sense if the farmer was old. Change one word, *"The farmer sold the cow because he needed money"*, and attention should now link **he** to **farmer**.

### The transformer

The **transformer**, introduced in 2017 (Chapter 2), is built from many layers. Each layer contains:

1. an **attention** step, where tokens gather information from each other, and
2. a small **neural network** step (Chapter 15) that processes each token's information.

Stacking dozens of these layers lets the model build up understanding step by step, much as image networks build edges into objects.

Why did transformers replace older designs such as RNNs? An RNN reads text **one token at a time**, like reading through a straw, so training is slow and it tends to forget the beginning of long texts. Attention looks at **all tokens at once**, so training can be split across many GPUs working in parallel. That efficiency is what allowed models to be scaled up to billions of parameters trained on enormous amounts of text.

## Step 4: predict, pick, repeat

After the last layer, the model gives a **probability** for every token in its vocabulary as the possible next token (using softmax, Chapter 15).

:::example Worked example: choosing the next token
Text so far: *"The best time to plant beans in Rwanda is during the…"*

| Possible next token | Probability (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| rainy | 0.72 |
| wet | 0.10 |
| season | 0.08 |
| morning | 0.03 |
| (all others) | 0.07 |

One token is chosen, added to the text, and the whole process repeats for the next token.
:::

A setting called **temperature** controls how the choice is made:

- **Low temperature** (near 0): almost always pick the most likely token. Answers are focused and repeatable, good for facts, code and data extraction.
- **Higher temperature**: give less likely tokens a bigger chance. Answers are more varied and creative, good for brainstorming and stories, but more likely to wander off or invent things.

This is why asking the same question twice can give different answers.

## How an LLM is trained

[[fig:llmstages|The three stages that turn a text predictor into a helpful assistant.]]

| Stage | What happens | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Pre-training** | Predict the next token on a huge amount of text from books, websites and code. This is **self-supervised** (Chapter 10): the text itself provides the answers | A model that knows a lot about language and the world, but simply continues text. Ask it a question and it might answer, or might write more questions |
| **2. Fine-tuning (instruction tuning)** | Train on many examples of good instructions and answers, written or checked by people | A model that **follows instructions** |
| **3. Preference tuning (such as RLHF)** | People rank several answers; the model is tuned towards preferred ones (Chapter 11) | More **helpful, polite and safer** answers |

Pre-training is by far the most expensive stage, needing thousands of GPUs for weeks or months. The later stages use much less data but shape the model's behaviour strongly.

## Important limits

Understanding how LLMs work explains their weaknesses.

- **Hallucinations.** The model predicts **likely** text, not **checked** truth. If a confident-sounding but false sentence is likely, it can write it: invented statistics, fake references, wrong dates. Important facts must be **checked** in reliable sources.
- **Knowledge cutoff.** It only knows what was in its training data, which ends at some date. It does not know about later events unless it is connected to search or given documents.
- **Context window.** It can only read a limited number of tokens at once (the question, the conversation so far and any documents). Beyond that, earlier text is cut off or not seen.
- **Bias.** It learned from human text, so it can repeat stereotypes and unfair patterns present in that text (Chapter 19).
- **Weaker in low-resource languages.** Less training text means lower quality, more tokens per sentence and more mistakes, for example in Kinyarwanda.
- **No real-world experience.** It has read about bicycles, but never ridden one. Its knowledge comes from text, not from acting in the world.

:::key Using LLMs well
Treat an LLM like a fast, well-read assistant who sometimes makes things up with confidence. Use it to draft, explain, summarise and brainstorm. **Check** facts, numbers and references; **never** paste confidential data into public tools (Chapter 21); and keep a person responsible for important decisions.
:::

### Reducing hallucinations with documents

A common technique is to give the model **trusted documents** together with the question, and instruct it to answer **only from them**. For example, a bank's chatbot is given the bank's official fee list before answering a fee question. This is called **retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**. It reduces hallucinations and keeps answers up to date, though checking is still wise.

## Beyond text

The same transformer ideas now power:

- **image generation** and image understanding,
- **speech recognition** and **text-to-speech**,
- **translation**,
- **multimodal** models that handle text, images and audio together (for example, describing a photo or answering questions about a chart).

:::mistake Common mistake
Thinking an LLM "looks up" answers in a database of facts. Unless it is connected to search or documents, it **generates** answers from patterns stored in its weights. That is why it can be fluent and wrong at the same time.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Try asking an assistant the same question in Kinyarwanda and in English. The English answer is often more detailed and accurate. This is not because Kinyarwanda is harder; it is because the model saw far less Kinyarwanda text during training. Building good Kinyarwanda datasets, with permission and care, is one of the most valuable AI contributions Rwanda can make.
:::

## Summary

- An LLM's basic job is to **predict the next token**, again and again. Doing this well at scale requires learning grammar, facts and reasoning patterns.
- Text becomes **tokens**, then **embeddings** (meaning as numbers). **Attention** lets each token use context from all the others.
- The **transformer** stacks attention and small networks in many layers and trains efficiently on GPUs.
- **Temperature** controls how adventurous the next-token choice is.
- Training stages: **pre-training**, **fine-tuning**, **preference tuning (RLHF)**.
- Limits: **hallucinations**, **knowledge cutoff**, **context window**, **bias**, weaker **low-resource languages**. Check important facts.

## Key terms

- **Large language model (LLM)** — a very large neural network trained to predict the next token of text.
- **Token** — a piece of text, such as a word, part of a word or punctuation, that a model processes.
- **Embedding** — a list of numbers representing the meaning of a token.
- **Attention** — a mechanism letting each token weigh the importance of other tokens for its meaning.
- **Transformer** — a neural network design built from layers of attention and small networks.
- **Temperature** — a setting controlling how random the choice of the next token is.
- **Pre-training** — the first, self-supervised stage of learning to predict the next token on huge amounts of text.
- **Instruction tuning** — fine-tuning a model on examples of instructions and good answers.
- **Hallucination** — a fluent, confident statement by an AI model that is false.
- **Knowledge cutoff** — the date after which a model has no training data.
- **Context window** — the maximum number of tokens a model can read at once.
- **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)** — giving a model trusted documents to answer from.
- **Multimodal model** — a model that handles several kinds of data, such as text, images and audio.

## Think deeper

1. In "The student gave the book to the teacher because she needed it", who is "she"? Explain how attention could help a model decide, and why the sentence is genuinely ambiguous.
:::answer
Most readers think "she" is the teacher, because the person who receives the book is usually the one who needed it. Attention lets "she" weigh "teacher", "book", "needed" and "gave" together and pick up this typical pattern from training. But the sentence is genuinely ambiguous: the student could also be "she" if, for example, the student needed the teacher to check the book. A model may choose the more common reading, which is a reason to write clearly when instructing AI systems.
:::
2. Why does an LLM sometimes invent a book reference that does not exist?
:::answer
It has seen very many real references, so it has learned what a reference looks like: author names, a plausible title, a year, a publisher. When asked for a reference, it generates text that fits this pattern with high probability, but it is not checking a library catalogue. The result can be a realistic-looking reference that does not exist. That is why references from an LLM must always be verified.
:::
3. A company wants its chatbot to answer questions about this year's prices. What two problems should it plan for, and what technique helps?
:::answer
First, the knowledge cutoff: the model's training data may not include this year's prices. Second, hallucination: it may confidently invent prices that sound plausible. Retrieval-augmented generation helps: give the chatbot the official, current price list with each question and instruct it to answer only from that document, and to say when the answer is not there.
:::

Every few years someone announces that machines will soon think like people. In 1965 a famous researcher predicted that within twenty years machines would be able to do any work a person can do. In 1970 another predicted human-level AI within "three to eight years". Neither came true.

At the same time, ideas that looked like failures, such as neural networks, quietly kept improving and, decades later, became the engines of the biggest technology products in the world.

This chapter tells that story. History is not decoration here. It gives you a practical skill: when you read a confident claim about AI today, you can ask whether it sounds like real progress or like the hype that has fooled people again and again.

## The beginning: can machines think? (1950–1956)

In **1950** the British mathematician **Alan Turing** published a paper called *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*. He asked a bold question: *can machines think?* Because "think" is hard to define, he replaced the question with a test he called the **imitation game**, now known as the **Turing Test**.

In the test, a judge has a typed conversation with two hidden partners: one a person, one a machine. If the judge cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine "passes". Turing's idea was practical: judge a machine by what it does, not by what is inside it. This is the same spirit as the definition you learned in Chapter 1.

:::think Think about it
Modern chatbots can often fool people in short conversations. Does that mean they "think"? Many researchers say the Turing Test measures how **human-like** a conversation is, not how **intelligent** or **truthful** a system is. A system can sound human and still be wrong. Keep this in mind whenever "it sounds smart" is used as proof.
:::

In the summer of **1956**, a group of researchers met at **Dartmouth College** in the United States. In the proposal for that workshop, **John McCarthy** used the term **"artificial intelligence"**. The workshop is seen as the birth of AI as a research field. Its founders were very optimistic: they believed that a few clever people working for one summer could make major progress on making machines use language, form ideas and improve themselves.

## The first wave and the first winter (1956–1980)

The early years produced exciting demonstrations:

- Programs that proved mathematical theorems.
- Programs that played draughts (checkers) and improved by playing.
- In **1958**, **Frank Rosenblatt** built the **perceptron**, an early artificial neuron that could learn simple patterns from examples. Newspapers described it as the start of machines that would walk, talk and be conscious.

These systems worked on small, clean "toy" problems. When researchers tried bigger, real problems, they hit walls:

1. **Computers were too slow and had too little memory.**
2. **There was too little data**, because most information was still on paper.
3. **Some methods had hard limits.** In 1969 a famous book showed that a single-layer perceptron cannot learn some very simple patterns. Interest in neural networks fell sharply for years.

When the big promises were not met, governments and companies cut funding in the **1970s**. This period is called the first **AI winter**: a time when interest, money and jobs in AI fell sharply.

## Expert systems and the second winter (1980–1993)

In the **1980s** AI came back, this time in business. **Expert systems** stored the knowledge of human experts as thousands of IF-THEN rules (the symbolic AI you met in Chapter 1). One famous system helped a computer company configure customer orders and was reported to save it millions of dollars a year. Companies built expert systems for medicine, banking and engineering.

But expert systems had serious problems:

- Writing the rules needed long interviews with busy experts.
- The rules **broke** when situations changed, and nobody could keep thousands of rules consistent.
- They could not learn; every change had to be written by hand.
- Special AI computers became expensive compared with ordinary personal computers.

By the late 1980s and early 1990s, much of the market collapsed. This was the **second AI winter**.

:::key Key idea
Hand-written rules do not **scale**. Each new situation needs new rules, and the rules start to conflict. This lesson pushed the field towards systems that **learn** rules from data.
:::

## Quiet progress and a famous chess match (1990s–2000s)

During the 1990s, AI research became more careful and more mathematical. Researchers borrowed tools from statistics and probability, and measured progress on shared test datasets. Machine learning slowly became practical in spam filters, handwriting readers for postal codes and cheques, and web search.

In **1997**, IBM's **Deep Blue** beat the world chess champion **Garry Kasparov** in a match. Deep Blue did not learn chess the way a person does. It used **search** (Chapter 5): it examined about **200 million positions per second**, using rules written with chess experts to judge positions. The lesson: **search plus very fast hardware** can beat human experts in a game with clear rules.

In the 2000s, two things grew enormously: the **internet** and later **smartphones**. Every search, photo, message and purchase created data. This data would soon change everything.

## The deep-learning revolution (2012)

Neural networks had never fully disappeared. A small group of researchers, including **Geoffrey Hinton**, **Yann LeCun** and **Yoshua Bengio**, kept improving them through the winters. (In 2018 the three received the Turing Award, often called the "Nobel Prize of computing", for this work.)

In **2012**, a deep neural network called **AlexNet**, built by Hinton's students, entered **ImageNet**, a yearly competition to recognise objects in more than a million photos across 1,000 categories. It won by a huge margin, making far fewer errors than the next team.

Why did it work now, when neural networks had disappointed before?

- **Data:** ImageNet gave it over a million labelled photos to learn from.
- **Computing power:** it was trained on **GPUs** (graphics processing units), chips designed for video games that happen to be perfect for the many small calculations neural networks need.
- **Better methods:** improvements such as better activation functions (Chapter 15) and ways to prevent overfitting (Chapter 13).

After 2012, deep learning spread quickly into speech recognition, translation, medical imaging and much more. This is often called the start of the **deep-learning era**.

## Learning beats champions and transformers arrive (2016–2017)

In **2016**, DeepMind's **AlphaGo** beat **Lee Sedol**, one of the world's best players of **Go**, a board game with far more possible positions than chess. Brute-force search alone could not work. AlphaGo combined **deep neural networks** that learned to judge positions with **search**, and improved by playing millions of games against itself (reinforcement learning, Chapter 11).

In **2017**, researchers at Google published a paper called *Attention Is All You Need*. It introduced the **transformer**, a neural network design built around a mechanism called **attention** (Chapter 17). Transformers could be trained efficiently on huge amounts of text using many GPUs at once. Nearly every modern language model, including those behind ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, is built on this design.

## AI becomes an everyday tool (2022 onwards)

In **November 2022**, OpenAI released **ChatGPT**, a chatbot built on a large language model. It reached millions of users within days and an estimated 100 million within about two months, one of the fastest adoptions of any technology. For the first time, ordinary people could use powerful AI simply by typing.

Since then, AI assistants have been built into search engines, phones, office software and customer-service systems around the world, including in Rwanda. The speed of this change is real, and so are its limits, which you will study in Week 4.

## Why progress came in waves

Look back over the whole story and a pattern appears. Every big jump needed **three ingredients at the same time**:

| Ingredient | What it means | Example of when it arrived |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** | Large collections of examples to learn from | The internet and smartphones (2000s); ImageNet (2009) |
| **Computing power** | Hardware fast enough to train big models | GPUs used for neural networks (around 2010 onwards) |
| **Better methods** | New ideas and designs | Deep networks (2012); transformers (2017) |

When one ingredient was missing, progress stalled and the money left: an AI winter. When all three arrived together after 2012, progress became very fast.

:::example Worked example: explaining an event with the three ingredients
**Event:** AlexNet wins ImageNet in 2012.
- **Data:** about 1.2 million labelled training images, far more than earlier projects had.
- **Computing:** two gaming GPUs made it possible to train a large network in days instead of months.
- **Methods:** a deeper convolutional network (Chapter 15) with techniques that reduced overfitting.
- **What it changed:** it convinced researchers and companies that deep learning works, starting a flood of investment.

You can analyse any AI milestone this way.
:::

:::mistake Common mistake
Believing that today's AI boom proves the hype is now always true. History shows that **real progress and over-promising usually happen together**. A useful habit: separate *what a system has been shown to do, under which conditions* from *what people predict it will do*.
:::

## AI in Africa and Rwanda

Africa is not only a user of AI built elsewhere. Some examples:

- **Zipline** has delivered blood and medical supplies by autonomous drone in Rwanda since **2016**, using software to plan flights and handle weather. It shows AI and automation solving a real problem in hilly terrain.
- Rwanda's Cabinet approved a **National AI Policy in 2023**, with goals for skills, data, infrastructure and responsible use (Chapter 22).
- Kigali hosts the **Centre for the Fourth Industrial Revolution (C4IR) Rwanda** and hosted the **Global AI Summit on Africa** in April 2025.
- Universities such as **Carnegie Mellon University Africa** and **AIMS** train AI talent in Kigali.
- Researchers and communities across the continent build data and tools for African languages, which big global models often handle poorly. Projects that collect recorded speech and text in Kinyarwanda, Swahili, Yoruba and other languages are helping close that gap.

:::rwanda In Rwanda
The three-ingredients idea applies locally too. Rwanda has invested in **connectivity** and **skills** (computing and methods). Its biggest challenge is often **data**: well-organised, representative, lawfully collected data, especially in Kinyarwanda. That is why the National AI Policy treats data as a priority.
:::

## Summary

- **1950:** Turing asks "can machines think?" and proposes the imitation game. **1956:** the Dartmouth workshop names the field "artificial intelligence".
- Early optimism met limits in computing, data and methods, causing the **first AI winter (1970s)**.
- **Expert systems** brought AI into business in the 1980s, but hand-written rules did not scale, causing the **second AI winter**.
- **1997:** Deep Blue beats Kasparov using search and fast hardware.
- **2012:** AlexNet starts the **deep-learning era**. **2016:** AlphaGo. **2017:** the **transformer**. **2022:** ChatGPT makes AI an everyday tool.
- Big jumps need **data, computing power and better methods together**. History warns us to be careful with hype.

## Key terms

- **Turing Test** — a test where a judge tries to tell a machine from a person through typed conversation; also called the imitation game.
- **Perceptron** — an early artificial neuron (1958) that learned simple patterns from examples.
- **AI winter** — a period when interest and funding in AI fell sharply after promises were not met.
- **GPU** — a graphics processing unit; a chip that does many small calculations at once, which makes training neural networks fast.
- **ImageNet** — a large collection of labelled photos used for a famous image-recognition competition.
- **Transformer** — the neural network design, introduced in 2017, behind modern language models.

## Think deeper

1. Why did the expert systems of the 1980s fail to keep their early success? Give two reasons.
:::answer
First, writing and maintaining thousands of hand-written rules was slow and expensive: every new situation needed new rules, and the rules started to conflict with each other. Second, expert systems could not learn; when the business or the world changed, people had to rewrite the rules by hand. They were also often tied to expensive special computers. Together this made them costly and fragile, so the market collapsed.
:::
2. Neural networks existed since 1958, yet they only became dominant after 2012. Use the three ingredients to explain why.
:::answer
In the early decades the method existed, but the other two ingredients were missing. There was not enough digital data to learn from, and computers were far too slow to train large networks. Some methods also had limits until researchers improved them. Around 2010–2012 all three came together: huge labelled datasets such as ImageNet (data), GPUs that trained networks quickly (computing power), and improved network designs and training tricks (methods). Only then could neural networks show their real strength.
:::
3. A start-up says: "Our AI will replace all doctors in five years." Using what you learned from AI history, write two sentences of advice to an investor.
:::answer
AI history is full of confident predictions of human-level ability within a few years that did not come true, so a bold timeline alone is a warning sign. Ask the start-up to show what its system has already been proven to do, on which patients and under which conditions, and invest based on that evidence rather than on the prediction.
:::

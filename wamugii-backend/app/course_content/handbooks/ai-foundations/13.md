Imagine a student who gets last year's exam paper and memorises every answer word for word. On last year's paper, they score 100%. On this year's paper, with new questions on the same topics, they fail. They **memorised**; they did not **learn**.

Machine-learning models can fall into exactly the same trap. A model that is perfect on the data it learned from may be useless on new data. This chapter explains how professionals catch this problem, and how they prevent it.

## Memorising is not learning

A model's job is to **generalise**: to perform well on **new** examples it has never seen, such as next month's loan applicants or next season's farms. Performance on the training data alone tells you very little, because a flexible model can simply memorise it.

:::key Key idea
The only honest measure of a model is how well it performs on data it **did not learn from**.
:::

## Three sets of data

Before training, professionals split their data into three separate parts.

[[fig:splits|One dataset, three separate jobs. The shares are typical, not fixed rules.]]

| Set | Typical share | Used for | Exam analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Training set** | about 70% | The model learns its patterns here | Studying from the textbook and exercises |
| **Validation set** | about 15% | Comparing models and tuning settings during development | Practice tests during the term |
| **Test set** | about 15% | One final, honest check at the very end | The sealed national exam |

### Why a separate validation set?

During development, a team tries many things: different methods, different features, different settings (such as the depth of a tree). Each time, they check the result on the **validation** set and keep what works best. This is useful, but it also means the team's choices slowly **fit** the validation set.

### Why the test set is opened only once

The **test set** is like the sealed national exam paper: you open it **once**, at the end. If you keep checking it and changing the model to improve the test score, you are slowly "teaching to the test", and the score stops being an honest estimate of real-world performance.

:::mistake Common mistake
Using the test set to choose between models, then reporting the same test score as "the accuracy". That number is now **too optimistic**. Choose with the validation set; report with the untouched test set.
:::

### Splitting carefully

How you split matters as much as the shares:

- **Shuffle randomly** when examples are independent, so each set is a fair sample.
- **Split by time** when the future must be predicted from the past (sales, weather, prices). Train on older data, test on newer data. A random split would let the model "see the future".
- **Keep related examples together.** If the same patient has five X-rays, all five must be in the same set; otherwise the model may recognise the patient rather than the disease.
- **Keep rare classes in every set.** If only 3% of loans are unpaid, make sure each set contains some unpaid loans (this is called a **stratified** split).

## Cross-validation

With a **small** dataset, holding back 30% for validation and testing leaves little to learn from. **Cross-validation** uses the data more efficiently:

1. Split the training data into, say, **5 equal parts** (called **folds**).
2. Train on 4 parts and validate on the 5th.
3. Rotate, so each part is used once for validation.
4. **Average** the five validation scores.

:::example Worked example: 5-fold cross-validation
A small clinic dataset has 500 patients. A model is trained five times, each time validated on a different 100 patients. Its five validation accuracies are **82%, 85%, 79%, 84% and 80%**.

Average = (82 + 85 + 79 + 84 + 80) ÷ 5 = 410 ÷ 5 = **82%**.

The spread (79% to 85%) also tells you something useful: the estimate is uncertain by a few points, so a rival model scoring 83% is not clearly better.
:::

## Underfitting, good fit and overfitting

[[fig:fitcurves|The same data with three models. The underfitting line misses the trend; the good fit follows it; the overfitting curve chases every point, including the noise.]]

| | Underfitting | Good fit | Overfitting |
|---|---|---|---|
| What happens | The model is too simple to capture the pattern | Captures the real pattern | Learns the noise and accidents in the training data |
| Training score | Poor | Good | Excellent |
| Validation score | Poor | Good, close to training | Poor, far below training |
| Exam analogy | Did not study | Understood the topic | Memorised last year's answers |

The tell-tale sign of **overfitting** is a **big gap**: a very high training score with a much lower validation score. The sign of **underfitting** is that **both** scores are low and close together.

:::example Worked example: reading the scores
| Model | Training accuracy | Validation accuracy | Diagnosis | Suggested action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 99% | 71% | **Overfitting** (gap of 28 points) | Simpler model, regularisation, more data |
| 2 | 62% | 60% | **Underfitting** (both low) | More useful features, a more flexible model |
| 3 | 88% | 86% | **Good fit** (both good, small gap) | Final check on the test set |
:::

### What counts as "low"?

Scores only mean something compared with a **baseline** (Chapter 8). If always predicting the most common answer already gives 60%, then a model with 62% has learned almost nothing. If the problem is very hard and the best human experts reach 70%, then 68% may be excellent.

## Why overfitting happens

- The model is **too complex** for the amount of data, for example a very deep decision tree on 200 rows. It has enough freedom to memorise every example.
- There is **too little data**, so random accidents look like patterns. With 20 loans, it might "learn" that everyone named Jean repays.
- **Training too long:** neural networks (Chapter 16) first learn general patterns, then, if training continues, start memorising.
- **Noisy labels:** the model tries to copy every label exactly, including the wrong ones.
- **Too many features** compared with examples.

## How to prevent overfitting

1. **Get more good data.** The most reliable fix: it is much harder to memorise 100,000 examples than 1,000.
2. **Use a simpler model,** or limit its complexity, for example by limiting a tree's depth.
3. **Regularisation:** add a penalty for complexity during training, so the model prefers simpler patterns unless complexity really helps.
4. **Early stopping:** while training, watch the validation score and stop when it stops improving.
5. **Dropout** (in neural networks): during training, randomly switch off some neurons, so the network cannot rely on any single path and must learn more general patterns.

Other helpful habits: remove useless features, use cross-validation to get reliable estimates, and for images, **data augmentation**: create extra training examples by slightly rotating, flipping or brightening photos.

## The bias–variance idea

Statisticians describe the same problem with two words:

- **High bias** = the model's assumptions are **too simple**, so it misses real patterns → **underfitting**. A straight line forced through a curved pattern has high bias.
- **High variance** = the model **changes a lot** when the training data changes a little → **overfitting**. A very deep tree trained on two slightly different samples may give completely different trees.

Good models **balance** the two: flexible enough to capture the real pattern, but not so flexible that they chase noise.

:::note Two meanings of "bias"
This statistical **bias** is different from the **unfair bias** you will study in Chapter 19, where a model treats some groups of people worse. Same word, different idea.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
A team builds a crop-yield model using data from 300 farms in one district over one good rainy season. It scores 97% on training data. Before celebrating, they should ask: was it tested on **unseen** farms? On a **different season**? In **other districts**? A model that memorised one season's farms can fail badly in a drought year. Splitting by **time** and **place** gives a much more honest picture.
:::

## Summary

- Always judge a model on data it has **not** seen. Generalising, not memorising, is the goal.
- **Training** set to learn, **validation** set to compare and tune, **test** set opened **once** at the end. Split by time when predicting the future, and keep related examples together.
- **Cross-validation** rotates the validation part to use small datasets well.
- A big gap between training and validation scores means **overfitting**; both scores low means **underfitting**.
- Prevent overfitting with more data, simpler models, regularisation, early stopping and dropout. **Bias** (too simple) and **variance** (too sensitive) must be balanced.

## Key terms

- **Generalisation** — a model's ability to perform well on new, unseen data.
- **Training set** — the data a model learns from.
- **Validation set** — data used during development to compare models and tune settings.
- **Test set** — data kept aside for one final, honest check.
- **Cross-validation** — rotating which part of the data is used for validation and averaging the results.
- **Fold** — one part of the data in cross-validation.
- **Stratified split** — a split that keeps the same share of each class in every set.
- **Overfitting** — learning noise in the training data, so performance on new data is poor.
- **Underfitting** — a model too simple to capture the real pattern.
- **Regularisation** — a penalty for complexity that pushes a model towards simpler patterns.
- **Early stopping** — ending training when the validation score stops improving.
- **Dropout** — randomly switching off neurons during training to reduce overfitting.
- **Bias (statistical)** — error from a model's assumptions being too simple.
- **Variance** — error from a model being too sensitive to the particular training data.

## Think deeper

1. A team predicting next month's electricity demand splits its two years of daily data randomly into training and test sets. Why is this a problem, and what should they do?
:::answer
A random split puts days from the end of the period into training and days from earlier into testing, so the model learns from the "future" relative to some test days. This makes test scores look better than real use, where only the past is available. They should split by time: train on the earlier months and test on the most recent months, which copies how the model will really be used.
:::
2. A deep decision tree gets 100% training accuracy and 64% validation accuracy. A tree limited to depth 4 gets 81% training and 79% validation. Which would you choose, and why?
:::answer
The depth-4 tree. The deep tree has memorised the training data (a 36-point gap shows strong overfitting) and performs worse on unseen data. The limited tree generalises much better, with a small gap and a higher validation score. Validation performance, not training performance, is what predicts real-world results.
:::
3. Why does getting more data reduce overfitting?
:::answer
With more examples, random accidents in individual examples cancel out, so they no longer look like patterns. A model that tries to memorise has to fit far more points, which is much harder, so it is pushed towards learning the real, general patterns that hold across many examples.
:::

Imagine a brilliant chef who is given rotten tomatoes. No recipe can save the meal. Machine learning works the same way: a model can only be as good as the data it learns from. Experienced teams often spend **most of a project's time** on data, finding it, cleaning it and checking it, and much less time on the model itself. A popular saying sums it up: **garbage in, garbage out**.

This chapter teaches you to look at data the way a professional does: what is in it, what is wrong with it, and what is quietly missing.

## Rows, features and labels

Most learning data can be pictured as a table. Imagine a savings cooperative that wants to predict whether a member will repay a small loan.

| Member | Age | Monthly sales (RWF) | Years in cooperative | Past late payments | **Repaid?** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 34 | 180,000 | 5 | 0 | **Yes** |
| B | 27 | 60,000 | 1 | 3 | **No** |
| C | 45 | 250,000 | 9 | 1 | **Yes** |
| D | 31 | 95,000 | 2 | 2 | **No** |

- Each **row** is one **example** (also called a record or an observation): one member.
- Each input column is a **feature**: a measurable property used to make the prediction (age, monthly sales, years, late payments).
- The column we want to predict is the **label**, also called the **target**: *Repaid?*

A supervised model learns how the features relate to the label in past examples, then predicts the label for **new** members whose outcome is not yet known.

:::key Key idea
**Features** are the clues; the **label** is the answer. A model is only useful if the features are available **at the moment the decision is made**.
:::

### How many rows and features?

There is no magic number, but some useful rules of thumb:

- More **rows** usually help, especially when patterns are complicated.
- Every extra **feature** needs more rows to learn about it reliably. A table with 50 rows and 40 features is a warning sign: the model may find accidental patterns (Chapter 13).
- **Rare outcomes need special care.** If only 2% of loans are not repaid, then 1,000 rows contain only about 20 examples of the thing you most want to predict.

## Types of data

| Type | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Numerical** | Numbers you can do maths with | Age, sales, temperature, rainfall in mm |
| **Categorical** | A choice from a list of names | District, crop type, phone brand, yes/no |
| **Text** | Words and sentences | SMS messages, product reviews, reports |
| **Images, audio, video** | Pixels and sound waves | Leaf photos, voice notes, CCTV, X-rays |
| **Time series** | Values recorded in time order | Daily rainfall, hourly electricity use, daily MoMo transactions |

**Categorical** data must usually be turned into numbers before a model can use it. A common way is **one-hot encoding**: the column *district* becomes several yes/no columns (*is_Gasabo*, *is_Huye*, *is_Musanze*…). Simply writing Gasabo = 1, Huye = 2, Musanze = 3 is risky, because the model may think Musanze is "three times" Gasabo.

### Structured and unstructured

- **Structured** data fits neatly into rows and columns, like the loan table.
- **Unstructured** data, such as text, images and sound, does not. It is richer but harder to use.

Older machine-learning methods work best on structured data. **Deep learning** (Week 3) made unstructured data usable at scale, which is why photos, speech and language became AI's biggest success stories.

## Seven common data-quality problems

| Problem | Example | Why it hurts | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Missing values** | Monthly sales empty for 20% of members | The model learns from an incomplete picture | Find the real values; fill with a sensible estimate; or add a "was missing" flag |
| **Errors and typos** | Age recorded as 340 | Impossible values distort patterns | Set valid ranges; check against the source |
| **Duplicates** | The same member entered twice | Some examples count double | Remove duplicates using a unique ID |
| **Inconsistent formats** | "Kigali", "kigali", "KGL" | One place looks like three | Standardise spelling and codes |
| **Wrong labels** | A repaid loan marked "No" | The model learns the wrong lesson | Double-check labels; use clear labelling rules |
| **Not representative** | Data only from urban members | The model fails for rural members | Collect data from every group the model will serve |
| **Out of date** | Prices from five years ago | The world has changed | Use recent data; plan regular updates |

The last two problems are the most **dangerous** because a quick look at the table does not reveal them. A dataset can be perfectly clean, with no typos or gaps, and still **not represent the people the model will serve**.

:::example Worked example: finding problems in a small table
| Member | District | Age | Monthly sales (RWF) | Repaid? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| E | Gasabo | 29 | 120,000 | Yes |
| F | gasabo | 41 | | Yes |
| G | Huye | 340 | 80,000 | No |
| E | Gasabo | 29 | 120,000 | Yes |

Problems found:
- **Inconsistent format:** "Gasabo" and "gasabo". Fix: standardise to "Gasabo".
- **Missing value:** member F's sales. Fix: look up the record; if impossible, fill with an estimate and add a "sales missing" flag.
- **Error:** member G's age 340 is impossible. Probably 34; check the original form.
- **Duplicate:** member E appears twice. Fix: keep one row.
- **Representativeness (cannot be seen in the table):** three of four rows are from Gasabo. Ask: will the model also be used in rural districts?
:::

## Data leakage: the hidden trap

**Leakage** happens when a feature secretly contains information about the answer that **would not be available at the time of the real decision**.

Example: a loan dataset includes a column "sent to debt collection". Of course it predicts non-repayment almost perfectly, because only unpaid loans are sent to debt collection. In testing, the model looks brilliant. In real life, when a new member applies, nobody knows yet whether they will be sent to debt collection, so the feature is empty and the model fails.

Other common forms of leakage:

- A hospital model to predict a disease uses the feature "medicine prescribed", but the medicine is prescribed **after** the diagnosis.
- A model to predict exam failure uses "attended the remedial class", which happens only **after** a student has failed.
- Information from the **test** data accidentally used during training (Chapter 13).

:::key The leakage question
For every feature, ask: **"Would I know this value at the exact moment I need the prediction?"** If the answer is no, remove it.
:::

:::mistake Common mistake
Celebrating a model that is "too good to be true", such as 99.9% accuracy on a hard problem. Very high scores are often a sign of leakage, not genius. Investigate before you celebrate.
:::

## Where data comes from, and permission

Data can come from internal records (sales, transactions), sensors, surveys, public datasets, partners or the internet. Always ask three questions:

1. **Who owns it,** and do we have **permission** to use it for this purpose?
2. Does it contain **personal data**? If so, data-protection law applies (Chapter 21).
3. **How was it collected,** and who might be missing from it?

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Rwanda's **Law No. 058/2021** on the protection of personal data and privacy means you cannot simply reuse personal data for any new purpose. If a cooperative collected phone numbers to send meeting reminders, using them to train a credit-scoring model is a **new purpose** that needs a lawful basis such as consent. Good data work starts with this question, not after the model is built.
:::

## Summary

- A model can only be as good as its data: **garbage in, garbage out**.
- Data is usually a table of **rows** (examples), **features** (inputs) and a **label** (the answer to predict).
- Data types: numerical, categorical, text, images and audio, time series. **Structured** data fits in tables; **unstructured** data does not.
- Seven common problems: missing values, errors, duplicates, inconsistent formats, wrong labels, **not representative**, **out of date**. The last two are the hardest to see.
- **Leakage** is a feature that would not be known at decision time. It makes models look great in testing and fail in real life.

## Key terms

- **Example (row)** — one record in a dataset, such as one member or one transaction.
- **Feature** — an input property used to make a prediction.
- **Label (target)** — the answer the model should predict.
- **Dataset** — the full collection of examples.
- **Categorical data** — values chosen from a list of names, such as district or crop type.
- **One-hot encoding** — turning a categorical column into several yes/no columns.
- **Structured data** — data that fits neatly into rows and columns.
- **Unstructured data** — text, images, audio and video, which do not fit neatly into columns.
- **Data cleaning** — fixing or removing errors, gaps, duplicates and inconsistencies.
- **Representative data** — data that matches the people and situations the model will meet.
- **Data leakage** — when a feature contains information about the answer that would not be available at decision time.

## Think deeper

1. A model to predict which students will drop out uses the feature "number of days absent this year", calculated at the end of the year. Is this leakage? How would you fix it?
:::answer
It is leakage if the prediction is meant to be made during the year, because the full-year absence count is only known at the end, often after the student has already dropped out. The fix is to use only information available at the decision moment, for example "days absent in the first term" if the prediction is made after the first term.
:::
2. A dataset of 5,000 crop photos is perfectly clean, but all photos were taken in good sunlight on research farms. What is the problem, and why can't cleaning fix it?
:::answer
The data is not representative of real use: farmers will take photos in shade, rain, with old phones and with weeds and soil in the picture. Cleaning fixes errors inside the data, but it cannot add the missing situations. The fix is to collect new photos in real farm conditions from the farmers and regions that will use the app.
:::
3. Why is coding districts as Gasabo = 1, Huye = 2, Musanze = 3 risky, and what is a better approach?
:::answer
Numbers suggest an order and distances that do not exist: a model may treat Musanze as "more" than Huye, or Gasabo and Huye as "closer" than Gasabo and Musanze. One-hot encoding avoids this by creating separate yes/no columns for each district, so no false order is introduced.
:::

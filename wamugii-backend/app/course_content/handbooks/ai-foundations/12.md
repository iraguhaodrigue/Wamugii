:::learn
- Write a complete, realistic plan for a machine-learning project, without writing code.
- Turn a vague idea into a clear decision with a numeric success measure.
- Choose and justify the learning type and problem type.
- Describe the data honestly: features, label, sources, quality, representativeness, leakage and permission.
- Plan a baseline, deployment, monitoring for drift, and the main risks.
:::

In real AI teams, the most valuable person is often not the one who writes the cleverest code, but the one who can say clearly **what should be built, why, with which data, and how we will know it worked**. This chapter is a project that trains exactly that skill.

You will write a **project plan of 2–3 pages** for one machine-learning idea that could help people in Rwanda or your own community. You will not write any code. You will think like the person who leads the project. As in Chapter 6, a complete **model plan** shows you the expected level of detail.

## Step 1: The problem

- **Who** has the problem? What **decision** do they make today, and **how**?
- What will the model **predict** or **find**?
- **Success measure:** write it as a **number**. For example: "find at least 80% of fraud cases while wrongly flagging fewer than 5% of normal transactions".
- Could a **simple rule** solve it without AI? Explain why machine learning is, or is not, needed.

## Step 2: Learning type and problem type

- **Supervised, unsupervised or reinforcement** learning? Why? (Chapters 9–11.)
- **Classification, regression, clustering, anomaly detection…?** Why? (Chapter 4.)

## Step 3: The data

Make a table of the features and the label:

| Feature / label | Type (number, category, text, image…) | Where it comes from | Possible quality problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

Then answer three questions:

- Is the data **representative** of everyone the model will serve?
- Is there any risk of **leakage** (a feature not known at decision time)?
- Do you have **permission** to use this data? Does it contain **personal information**?

## Step 4: Method

Name one simple **baseline** method (for example a rule, logistic regression or a small decision tree) and one **stronger** method you might try later (for example a random forest). Explain the trade-off: accuracy against cost, speed and how easy it is to explain.

## Step 5: Deployment and monitoring

- How will the user **actually receive** the result (SMS, app, dashboard, printed report)?
- What could cause **drift** in your problem?
- How often will you **check and retrain**?

## Step 6: Risks

Name **two** things that could go wrong (wrong predictions, unfair results for one group, privacy problems, misuse) and how you would **reduce** each one.

## Model plan: supporting pupils at risk of dropping out

:::example Model plan, Step 1: the problem
**Who:** head teachers and the sector education officer in one district. Today, they notice pupils at risk only when the pupil has already stopped coming, usually too late to help.

**Prediction:** at the end of the **first term** of Primary 6, estimate which pupils are at **high risk** of not finishing the year, so that teachers can visit families and offer support early (mentoring, school-feeding follow-up, help with materials).

**Success measure:** among pupils who would really drop out, the model flags **at least 70%** (recall ≥ 70%), while flagging no more than **15% of all pupils**, because teachers can only follow up that many.

**Why not a simple rule?** A rule such as "flag pupils absent more than 10 days" is a good **baseline** and might be enough. ML is worth testing because dropout probably depends on **combinations** of factors (absence **and** falling marks **and** distance from school) that a single rule misses. If ML does not clearly beat the rule, we keep the rule.
:::

:::example Model plan, Steps 2 and 3: learning type and data
**Learning type:** **supervised**, because past school records show which pupils actually finished the year (the label). **Problem type:** **binary classification** (will finish / at risk), with a risk probability so schools can rank pupils.

| Feature / label | Type | Source | Possible quality problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days absent in term 1 | Number | School attendance register | Paper registers, missing weeks |
| Change in marks, P5 to term 1 of P6 | Number | School records | Different marking across schools |
| Distance from home to school | Number | Pupil records or sector data | Approximate or out of date |
| Repeated a year before | Category (yes/no) | School records | Not always recorded |
| School-feeding participation | Category | School-feeding records | Different schools record differently |
| **Label: finished P6 that year** | Category (yes/no) | End-of-year records, past 3 years | Transfers wrongly counted as dropouts |

**Representative?** Records from only urban schools would not serve rural pupils. We need data from **all** school types in the district, rural and urban, public and private.

**Leakage check:** "attended a remedial class" happens **after** a pupil is identified as struggling, and "term 3 attendance" is not known at the end of term 1. Both are **excluded**.

**Permission and privacy:** these are records about **children**, which is sensitive personal data. We need approval from the district and the ministry, a clear purpose, minimal data (no names in the training data, only pseudonymous IDs), secure storage in Rwanda and compliance with Law No. 058/2021 (Chapter 21).
:::

:::example Model plan, Steps 4 to 6: method, deployment and risks
**Baseline:** the absence rule ("flag if more than 10 days absent in term 1"). **Stronger method:** logistic regression, which gives a probability and shows which factors raise the risk, important for teachers' trust. A random forest only if it is clearly better on validation data.

**Deployment:** a one-page printed list per school after term 1, sorted by risk, with the main reason for each pupil ("many absences, marks falling"), discussed in a staff meeting. No pupil is labelled publicly.

**Drift:** a new school-feeding policy, a change in fees, a new school opening nearby, or a drought affecting family income could change the patterns. We compare predictions with real outcomes every year and retrain annually.

**Risk 1, labelling pupils unfairly:** a "high risk" label could lower teachers' expectations. **Reduce it:** the list is used only to **offer support**, never to punish or exclude, and teachers are trained on this.

**Risk 2, unequal accuracy:** the model may miss at-risk pupils in schools with poor records. **Reduce it:** check recall **separately** for rural and urban schools and for girls and boys before use, and improve record-keeping where accuracy is low.
:::

Notice what makes this plan strong: a clear **user** and **decision**, a numeric **success measure** tied to real capacity (15% of pupils), an honest **baseline**, a data table with specific **quality risks**, an explicit **leakage** check, serious attention to **privacy** for children's data, and **risks with concrete fixes**.

:::mistake Common mistakes in this project
- A vague goal ("use AI to improve education") with no decision and no number.
- Forgetting the baseline, so nobody can judge whether ML helps.
- Listing features without asking whether they are known **at decision time**.
- Ignoring permission and privacy, especially for children, patients or financial data.
- Deployment that ignores how the user really works ("a mobile app" for teachers with no smartphones).
:::

- Detect unusual electricity use that may mean a faulty meter.

## Checklist

- Every step is answered in clear, simple sentences.
- The success measure is a **number**, linked to a real decision or capacity.
- The data table has **at least four features** and the label, with sources and quality risks.
- Leakage, representativeness and permission are checked explicitly.
- Risks are honest and specific, each with a way to reduce it.

## Summary

- A strong ML plan names the **user and decision**, a **numeric success measure**, the **learning and problem type**, **representative data with permission**, a **simple baseline first**, **deployment that fits real work**, a plan for **drift**, and specific **risks with fixes**.
- Planning well is the most valuable skill in a real AI team, and it needs no code.

## Key terms

- **Project plan** — a written description of what will be built, why, with which data and how success will be judged.
- **Success measure** — a number that defines when the project has worked.
- **Pseudonymous ID** — a code that replaces a person's name in data so they are not directly identified.

## Think deeper

1. In the model plan, why is the success measure tied to "no more than 15% of all pupils flagged"?
:::answer
Because teachers have limited time. A model that flags 50% of pupils might catch nearly every real dropout, but teachers could not follow up so many families, so the list would be ignored. Linking the measure to real capacity makes sure the model is useful in practice, not just accurate on paper.
:::
2. Why does the model plan choose logistic regression over a possibly more accurate random forest?
:::answer
Teachers and education officers need to trust and understand the result, and logistic regression shows clearly which factors raise a pupil's risk. It also gives a probability for ranking. A random forest is only worth its extra complexity if it is clearly better on validation data. For a sensitive decision about children, explainability matters as much as a small gain in accuracy.
:::
3. Give one more example of leakage that could appear in a dropout model.
:::answer
"Number of home visits by teachers this year" would be leakage if visits are made because a pupil is already known to be struggling or absent; the visits are a result of the problem, not information available before it. Another example: "transferred to another school" recorded at year end, which is only known after the outcome.
:::

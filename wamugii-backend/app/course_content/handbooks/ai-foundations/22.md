Cars are powerful and useful, and they can kill. So societies built **rules around them**: driving licences, speed limits, vehicle inspections, insurance, traffic police. Nobody thinks these rules "stop cars"; they make cars safe enough to use everywhere.

AI is going through the same process now. **AI governance** is the set of rules, roles and processes that make sure AI is developed and used **safely, fairly, lawfully and for the public good**. This chapter shows you the main rules being built around the world and in Rwanda, and how to write sensible rules for your own organisation.

## Three levels of governance

1. **International:** shared principles agreed between countries and organisations.
2. **National:** each country's laws, policies and regulators.
3. **Organisational:** a company's, school's or ministry's own rules for how it builds and uses AI.

Most people will never write a law, but almost everyone who works with AI will help shape level 3.

## International principles

- **OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024).** Adopted by many countries. AI should benefit people and the planet, respect human rights and democratic values, be transparent and explainable, be robust, secure and safe, and have clear accountability.
- **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021).** Adopted by UNESCO's member states, it was the first global standard on AI ethics. It focuses on human rights and dignity, fairness, environmental impact, diversity and inclusion, and includes practical tools for countries to assess their readiness.
- **African Union Continental AI Strategy (2024).** An African approach that stresses using AI for **development**, building **skills** and infrastructure, **inclusion**, and developing **African data and languages**, while managing risks.

These principles are not laws by themselves, but they guide the laws and policies countries write.

## Rwanda's National AI Policy

Rwanda's Cabinet approved a **National Artificial Intelligence Policy in 2023**, developed by the **Ministry of ICT and Innovation** with partners including **C4IR Rwanda**. It aims to position Rwanda as a leader in **responsible and inclusive AI** in Africa, using AI to support the country's development goals. Its priority areas include:

| Priority area | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Skills** | AI literacy and 21st-century skills from schools to universities, and training for public servants |
| **Infrastructure and computing** | The connectivity, data centres and computing power AI needs |
| **Data** | A strategy to make quality data available for AI while protecting privacy |
| **Public sector** | Trustworthy AI to improve government services |
| **Private sector** | Helping businesses adopt AI and grow an AI industry |
| **Ethics** | Practical guidelines for the responsible, ethical use of AI |

The policy works together with the **data-protection law** (Law No. 058/2021, Chapter 21) and the work of the **National Cyber Security Authority**. Together they form Rwanda's foundation for AI governance.

## The EU AI Act: a risk-based model

The European Union's **AI Act** entered into force in **August 2024**, with its rules applying in stages from 2025 onwards. It is the world's first comprehensive AI law. It matters to African companies too: anyone offering AI products or services in the EU must follow it, and other countries look to it as a model.

Its core idea is to **regulate according to risk**: the more an AI system can affect people's safety and rights, the stricter the rules.

[[fig:riskpyramid|The EU AI Act's four risk levels. Most AI systems are minimal risk; the strictest rules apply to the few at the top.]]

| Risk level | Examples | What the law requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Unacceptable** | Social scoring of citizens by public authorities; systems that manipulate people or exploit vulnerable groups such as children; certain uses of real-time facial recognition in public spaces | **Banned**, with narrow exceptions |
| **High** | AI for hiring and managing workers, credit scoring, access to education and exam scoring, medical devices, critical infrastructure, essential public services | Strict duties: risk management, high-quality data, documentation, record-keeping, transparency, **human oversight**, accuracy and security |
| **Limited** | Chatbots, AI-generated or manipulated content (deepfakes) | **Transparency**: tell people they are dealing with AI; label deepfakes |
| **Minimal** | Spam filters, AI in video games, most everyday AI | No special duties (voluntary codes encouraged) |

The Act also sets rules for providers of **general-purpose AI models** (such as large language models), including documentation and, for the most powerful models, extra safety duties.

:::note Dates change
The AI Act's obligations apply in stages, and the timing of some rules has been under discussion. When you need exact dates, check the official EU sources.
:::

:::example Worked example: classifying systems by risk level
| System | Risk level | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| A CV-screening tool that ranks job applicants | **High** | Affects people's access to employment |
| A customer-service chatbot for a telecom company | **Limited** | Users must be told they are talking to an AI |
| An email spam filter | **Minimal** | Little effect on people's rights or safety |
| A credit-scoring app for personal loans | **High** | Affects access to essential financial services |
| A system that scores citizens' general behaviour to decide access to public services | **Unacceptable** | Social scoring is banned |
| An AI that marks national exam essays | **High** | Affects access to education and people's futures |
:::

The risk-based idea is useful everywhere, not just in Europe: **the more an AI system can affect people's rights and safety, the more care and control it needs.**

## Governance inside an organisation

Every organisation that uses AI, whether a school, a bank, a clinic, a start-up or a ministry, should have simple written rules. Six core rules:

1. **AI use policy.** Which AI tools staff may use, and for what tasks.
2. **Data rules.** What information must **never** go into external AI tools (customer data, patient data, passwords, unpublished financial results).
3. **Risk review.** High-impact uses, affecting people's money, jobs, health, education or rights, need extra checks before launch: an **impact assessment**, fairness testing and approval by a named manager.
4. **Human accountability.** A **named person** is responsible for each AI system: its performance, its problems and its decisions.
5. **Transparency.** Tell customers when AI is used, explain important decisions, and always provide a way to reach a **human**.
6. **Monitoring and incident reporting.** Track performance and complaints; when something goes wrong, record it, fix it and learn from it.

### The impact assessment

Before launching a high-impact system, answer in writing:

- What is the **purpose**, and what decision does the system support?
- **Who is affected**, and how could they be harmed?
- What **data** is used, and on what lawful basis?
- How was it **tested**, including **per-group** results?
- How do **people stay in control** (who reviews, who can override)?
- How can affected people **complain or appeal**?
- What would make us **stop or pause** the system?
- When will we **review** this assessment?

:::mistake Common mistake
Writing an AI policy that is so long and vague that nobody reads it. Good governance is **short, specific and used**: six clear rules staff actually know beat forty pages in a drawer.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
A small business in Kigali does not need a legal department to govern AI well. A one-page policy that says which tools are allowed, what data must never be pasted into them, who is responsible for each AI tool, and how customers can reach a person already puts it ahead of many large companies, and supports the goals of the National AI Policy and the data-protection law.
:::

## Summary

- **AI governance** = rules, roles and processes for safe, fair, lawful and beneficial AI, at **international, national** and **organisational** levels.
- International principles: **OECD** (2019), **UNESCO** (2021), **African Union** Continental AI Strategy (2024).
- **Rwanda's National AI Policy (2023)** prioritises skills, infrastructure, data, public sector, private sector and ethics, alongside the data-protection law.
- The **EU AI Act** (in force since August 2024) regulates by risk: **unacceptable** (banned), **high** (strict duties), **limited** (transparency), **minimal** (no special duties).
- Every organisation needs short, clear rules: use policy, data rules, risk review, human accountability, transparency, monitoring.

## Key terms

- **AI governance** — rules, roles and processes that keep AI safe, fair, lawful and beneficial.
- **OECD AI Principles** — international principles for trustworthy AI adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024.
- **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI** — the first global standard on AI ethics, adopted in 2021.
- **African Union Continental AI Strategy** — the African Union's 2024 strategy for AI on the continent.
- **National AI Policy (Rwanda)** — Rwanda's 2023 policy for responsible, inclusive AI.
- **EU AI Act** — the European Union's law regulating AI by level of risk.
- **Risk-based regulation** — stricter rules for systems that can cause more harm.
- **High-risk AI system** — under the EU AI Act, a system with strict duties because it affects safety or rights.
- **Social scoring** — rating people's general behaviour to decide their access to services; banned under the EU AI Act.
- **Impact assessment** — a written analysis of an AI system's purpose, risks and controls before launch.
- **Accountability** — a named person being responsible for an AI system and its effects.

## Think deeper

1. Why does the EU AI Act matter to a Rwandan start-up that only has customers in Rwanda today?
:::answer
If the start-up later wants to serve European customers or partners, it must follow the Act, and building the right practices from the start is cheaper than changing later. Investors and large partners often expect similar standards. And the Act's risk-based ideas are simply good practice: a high-impact system deserves strong testing, documentation and human oversight wherever it is used.
:::
2. A university plans to use AI to rank scholarship applicants. Which risk level would this be under the EU AI Act, and name three duties that would follow?
:::answer
It would be high risk, because it affects access to education. Duties would include a risk-management process, high-quality and representative training data with documentation, and effective human oversight so that people review and can override the ranking. Record-keeping, transparency to applicants and accuracy testing would also apply.
:::
3. Write a one-sentence rule for staff about using public chatbots, and explain why it matters.
:::answer
"Never paste personal data about customers, patients or staff, passwords, or confidential company information into any AI tool that the organisation has not approved for that data." It matters because text entered into public tools may be stored or processed outside the organisation's control, which can break data-protection law and expose people and the organisation to harm.
:::

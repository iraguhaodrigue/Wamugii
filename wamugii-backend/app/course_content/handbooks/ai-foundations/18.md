This chapter is the Week 3 project. You will play the role of a **junior analyst** at a health organisation in Rwanda. Two teams have built models, and your manager asks a short question with big consequences:

> *"Which model should we use, and is it ready?"*

You will write a **1–2 page evaluation report**. A complete **model report** at the end of the chapter shows the expected level of thinking. Try the steps yourself before reading it.

## The situation

The organisation wants to predict which pregnant women are at **high risk** of complications, so that they can be offered **extra clinic visits**. Both models were tested on the **same test set of 1,000 women**, of whom **100 are truly high-risk**.

**Model A** (a large neural network)

| | Truly high-risk | Truly low-risk |
|---|---|---|
| Predicted high-risk | 70 | 30 |
| Predicted low-risk | 30 | 870 |

Training accuracy: 99%. Validation accuracy: 91%.

**Model B** (a random forest)

| | Truly high-risk | Truly low-risk |
|---|---|---|
| Predicted high-risk | 88 | 112 |
| Predicted low-risk | 12 | 788 |

Training accuracy: 89%. Validation accuracy: 87%.

## Step 1: Calculate

For each model, calculate **accuracy, precision and recall**. Show your working, using the formulas from Chapter 14:

- Accuracy = (TP + TN) ÷ total
- Precision = TP ÷ (TP + FP)
- Recall = TP ÷ (TP + FN)

:::tip Read the matrix carefully
In these tables, **TP** is "predicted high-risk and truly high-risk" (top left), **FP** is "predicted high-risk but truly low-risk" (top right), **FN** is "predicted low-risk but truly high-risk" (bottom left) and **TN** is bottom right.
:::

## Step 2: Check for overfitting

Compare training and validation accuracy for each model (Chapter 13). Which shows signs of **overfitting**? What would you suggest to that team?

## Step 3: Choose the measure that matters

- What does a **false negative** mean here? (A high-risk woman **not** given extra care.)
- What does a **false positive** mean? (A low-risk woman given extra visits she did not need.)
- Which mistake is more costly? So which measure matters most?

## Step 4: Consider real-world limits

Models do not work in empty space. A clinic has a limited number of staff and appointment slots. Ask: **how many referrals would each model create**, and can the clinics handle them?

## Step 5: Recommend

Write one paragraph: which model, why, and what must happen **before** it is used with real patients.

## Step 6: One-slide summary

Make one slide, or half a page, that a busy manager can read in one minute: the choice, three key numbers and one risk.

## Model evaluation report

:::example Model report, calculations
**Model A:** TP = 70, FP = 30, FN = 30, TN = 870.
- Accuracy = (70 + 870) ÷ 1,000 = 940 ÷ 1,000 = **94%**.
- Precision = 70 ÷ (70 + 30) = 70 ÷ 100 = **70%**.
- Recall = 70 ÷ (70 + 30) = 70 ÷ 100 = **70%**.

**Model B:** TP = 88, FP = 112, FN = 12, TN = 788.
- Accuracy = (88 + 788) ÷ 1,000 = 876 ÷ 1,000 = **87.6%**.
- Precision = 88 ÷ (88 + 112) = 88 ÷ 200 = **44%**.
- Recall = 88 ÷ (88 + 12) = 88 ÷ 100 = **88%**.

| | Model A | Model B |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | 94% | 87.6% |
| Precision | 70% | 44% |
| Recall | 70% | 88% |
| High-risk women missed (FN) | 30 | 12 |
| Women referred (TP + FP) | 100 | 200 |
:::

:::example Model report, overfitting and costs
**Overfitting:** Model A drops from 99% (training) to 91% (validation), an **8-point gap**, a sign of overfitting. Model B drops from 89% to 87%, a small 2-point gap, so it generalises more consistently. Suggestion for Team A: regularisation, dropout or early stopping, and more training data, then re-test.

**Cost of mistakes:** a **false negative** means a high-risk woman receives no extra care. Complications such as pre-eclampsia or severe bleeding can be life-threatening if not caught early. A **false positive** means a low-risk woman makes extra clinic visits: a cost in time, transport and staff, but rarely dangerous. The false negative is the more serious mistake, so **recall** matters most, while precision must stay high enough that clinics can cope.

**What the numbers mean in people:** for every 1,000 women, Model A misses **30** high-risk women; Model B misses **12**. That is 18 more high-risk women receiving extra care with Model B. But Model B also refers 112 low-risk women, against 30 for Model A.
:::

:::example Model report, recommendation
**Recommendation:** Model B is the better starting point for this screening task, because it catches far more high-risk women (recall 88% against 70%) and does not show signs of overfitting. Its higher accuracy makes Model A look better at first glance, but accuracy hides the 30 missed high-risk women.

**Model B is not ready for patients yet.** Before use: (1) confirm that clinics can handle about **200 referrals per 1,000 women**, or adjust its threshold (see below); (2) test it on data from **more districts**, including rural health centres; (3) check recall **separately** for different groups, such as rural and urban women, younger and older women, and first and later pregnancies (Chapter 19); (4) a **health worker always makes the final decision**, using the model as a support, not a replacement; (5) plan monitoring of real outcomes after launch.

**If clinics can handle only 150 referrals per 1,000 women:** Model B at its current threshold (200 referrals) is over capacity, while Model A (100 referrals) fits but misses 30 high-risk women. The best option is to **raise Model B's threshold** so that it refers about 150 women, then re-measure its recall on the validation set. If its recall stays well above 70%, it is still the better choice. If the remaining capacity cannot be stretched, the organisation should also ask whether extra visits can be shorter or combined with routine visits, so that more high-risk women can be seen.
:::

:::example Model report, one-slide summary
**Choice:** Model B (random forest), after local testing and threshold tuning.
**Key numbers:** catches **88 of 100** high-risk women (Model A: 70); refers **200 per 1,000** women; validation accuracy **87%** with no sign of overfitting.
**Main risk:** clinic overload from false alarms. Mitigation: tune the threshold to clinic capacity and keep a health worker in charge of every decision.
:::

Notice how the report is built: **numbers with working**, a clear **overfitting check**, mistakes translated into **real consequences for people**, a decision that respects **capacity**, and concrete **conditions before launch**. It does not simply pick the highest accuracy.

:::mistake Common mistakes in this project
- Choosing Model A "because 94% is higher than 87.6%".
- Mixing up FP and FN cells in the table.
- Forgetting that "ready for patients" needs more than good test numbers: wider testing, fairness checks and human oversight.
- Ignoring capacity: a model that sends 500 referrals to a clinic that can see 100 will be ignored in practice.
:::

## Summary

- Good evaluation is not "pick the highest accuracy". Compute the **right measures**, check for **overfitting**, link errors to **real costs**, and respect **real limits** like capacity.
- In health screening, **recall** usually matters most, but precision decides the workload.
- The **threshold** can be tuned to match capacity; always re-measure after changing it.
- A model is ready for real people only after wider testing, per-group checks, human oversight and a monitoring plan.

## Key terms

- **Evaluation report** — a written assessment of a model's performance with a recommendation.
- **Referral** — sending a person for extra care or checks because of a model's prediction.
- **Capacity** — how many cases people can actually handle, which limits how many alerts a model should create.
- **Human oversight** — keeping a person responsible for decisions a model supports.

## Think deeper

1. A colleague says: "Model B has 112 false positives, so it is worse." How would you respond?
:::answer
The false positives are real costs, but they must be weighed against what they buy: Model B catches 18 more high-risk women than Model A. In maternal health, missing a high-risk woman can be fatal, while an extra visit is an inconvenience. So more false positives can be an acceptable price, as long as the clinics can handle the workload. The right comparison is the cost of each kind of mistake, not just the count.
:::
2. If Model B's threshold is raised so that it refers 150 women, what do you expect to happen to its precision and recall, and why must you re-measure?
:::answer
Raising the threshold means the model flags only the cases it is most confident about, so precision will usually rise (fewer false alarms among the referrals) and recall will usually fall (some high-risk women drop below the new threshold). The exact new values cannot be calculated from the current table, because we do not know the probabilities of each case, so they must be re-measured on the validation set.
:::
3. Why should the model be tested separately for rural and urban women before launch?
:::answer
A model can perform well on average while performing poorly for one group, for example if rural women were under-represented in the training data or their records are less complete. If recall is much lower for rural women, the model would miss more high-risk women exactly where health services are already harder to reach. Checking each group separately reveals this before it causes harm.
:::

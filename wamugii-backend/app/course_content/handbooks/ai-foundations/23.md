People often imagine that working in AI means being a mathematics genius writing algorithms alone in a laboratory. A few people do that. But a real AI product, such as a fraud system for a bank or a crop-disease app, needs a whole team: people who understand the problem, people who manage data, people who build software, people who check fairness and law, and people who explain it to users.

Many of the most valuable people combine **understanding of AI** with **deep knowledge of a field** such as health, farming, finance, law or education. You have just spent four weeks building exactly that understanding. This chapter helps you decide where to take it.

## Technical roles

| Role | What they do day to day | Key skills |
|---|---|---|
| **Data analyst** | Cleans data, makes charts and reports, answers business questions with numbers | Excel or Google Sheets, SQL, statistics, visualisation, clear writing |
| **Data scientist** | Frames problems, builds and evaluates models, explains results to managers | Python, statistics, machine learning, evaluation (Chapter 14), communication |
| **Machine-learning engineer** | Turns models into reliable software that runs at scale; monitors drift | Python, software engineering, cloud, MLOps (deployment and monitoring) |
| **Data engineer** | Builds the pipelines that collect, store, clean and move data | SQL, databases, cloud services, programming |
| **AI / LLM application developer** | Builds apps and assistants on top of language models; designs prompts and evaluates outputs | Programming, APIs, prompt design, retrieval (RAG), testing and evaluation |
| **AI researcher** | Invents and tests new methods; publishes papers | Advanced mathematics, deep learning, research methods; usually a master's or PhD |

## Hybrid and non-technical roles

- **AI product manager:** decides **what** to build and **why**, for real users; keeps the team focused on the decision being improved (Chapter 8).
- **AI ethics, policy and governance specialist:** fairness testing, privacy, impact assessments and regulation (Chapters 19–22). Demand is growing as laws like the EU AI Act take effect.
- **Data annotator / data-quality lead:** creates, checks and manages labelled data (Chapter 9). Quality leads design labelling rules and check consistency.
- **AI trainer / educator:** teaches teams and students to use AI well and safely.
- **Domain expert using AI:** a nurse, agronomist, accountant, lawyer, journalist or teacher who uses AI tools to work better and helps AI teams understand their field. **This is the largest group of all**, and it is growing fastest.

:::key Key idea
You do not have to choose between "AI career" and "my field". **AI + your field** is often the strongest position: a nurse who understands model evaluation, or an agronomist who can plan an ML project, is rare and valuable.
:::

## Skills that last

Tools change every year: today's popular app or library may be replaced in two years. These skills keep their value:

1. **Problem framing:** turning a real need into a clear AI problem with a decision and a numeric success measure (Weeks 1–2).
2. **Data thinking:** knowing what makes data good, representative and lawful (Chapter 7).
3. **Evaluation:** judging results honestly with the right measures and baselines (Week 3).
4. **Responsible AI:** fairness, explainability, privacy, security and governance (Week 4).
5. **Communication:** explaining AI to non-experts in simple words, in writing and speech.
6. **Python and statistics:** the foundation of every technical path.
7. **Learning how to learn:** reading documentation, testing new tools carefully, and updating your knowledge.

## Build a portfolio, not just certificates

Certificates show you studied. A **portfolio** shows you can **do the work**. Employers and clients want proof.

A strong beginner portfolio has:

- **2–3 small projects on real local problems.** Your Week 1, 2, 3 and final projects from this course are a strong start.
- **Clear write-ups** for each project: the problem, the data, the method, the results (with honest numbers), the limits and what you would do next.
- **A public profile:** GitHub for code (once you start coding), LinkedIn for your story and certificates, or a simple personal web page.

:::example Worked example: turning a course project into a portfolio piece
**Title:** "Planning an early-warning model for primary-school dropout in one district."
**Problem (2 sentences):** schools notice at-risk pupils too late; the goal is a list after term 1 so teachers can offer support.
**Approach:** supervised classification; features from attendance and marks; logistic regression baseline; recall target of 70% within a 15% flag limit.
**Responsible AI:** leakage checks, privacy for children's data, per-group recall checks for rural and urban schools.
**What I learned:** linking the success measure to teachers' real capacity changed the whole design.
**Next step:** test the plan with two head teachers and collect one term of anonymised data with permission.

This is one page, but it shows problem framing, data thinking, evaluation and responsibility, which is exactly what employers look for.
:::

:::mistake Common mistake
Collecting many online certificates without building anything. Ten certificates with no projects are weaker than one certificate and two well-written projects. Learn, then **apply** immediately.
:::

## Summary

- AI teams need **technical roles** (analyst, data scientist, ML engineer, data engineer, LLM developer, researcher), **hybrid roles** (product, ethics and governance, data quality, education) and **domain experts using AI**, the largest group.
- Lasting skills: **problem framing, data thinking, evaluation, responsible AI, communication, Python and statistics**, and learning how to learn.
- Rwanda's ecosystem includes universities, innovation hubs, policy bodies, events and online communities.
- Build a **portfolio** of real projects with clear write-ups; it proves more than certificates alone.

## Key terms

- **Data analyst** — a person who cleans data and turns it into reports and insights.
- **Data scientist** — a person who frames problems and builds and evaluates models.
- **Machine-learning engineer** — a person who turns models into reliable software and keeps them running well.
- **Data engineer** — a person who builds systems to collect, store and move data.
- **MLOps** — practices for deploying, monitoring and maintaining machine-learning models.
- **AI product manager** — a person who decides what AI product to build and why, for real users.
- **Portfolio** — a collection of real projects that shows what you can do.

2. Why might a nurse who understands AI be more valuable to a health-AI project than an extra programmer?
:::answer
The nurse knows how clinics really work: which decisions matter, which mistakes are dangerous, what data is reliable, and how staff will actually use a tool. That knowledge shapes the problem definition, the choice of success measure (for example recall), the features and the deployment, which are the stages where most AI projects fail. A programmer can be hired more easily than this field knowledge combined with AI understanding.
:::

This chapter is a project. You will analyse one real AI system using every tool from Week 1: the definition and the three circles (Chapter 1), history and the three ingredients (Chapter 2), agents, PEAS and environments (Chapter 3), problem types (Chapter 4) and search (Chapter 5).

The result is a short report of about **two pages** (or 8–10 slides), the kind of clear analysis a company expects from a junior analyst. To show you what "good" looks like, this chapter includes a complete **model analysis** of drone delivery in Rwanda. Read it carefully, then write your own on a different system.

## Step 1: Choose your system

Choose a system you can learn about from public sources (websites, articles, videos) or from people who use it. Ideas:

- Drone delivery of medical supplies.
- Mobile-money fraud detection.
- A crop-disease app that reads leaf photos.
- A Kinyarwanda speech-recognition or translation tool.
- A chatbot used by a bank, telecom company or government service.
- A recommendation feed (YouTube, TikTok, an online shop).
- Face unlock on a smartphone.

:::tip Choosing well
Pick something specific ("the fraud alerts on my mobile-money account") rather than something vague ("AI in banking"). A specific system is easier to describe precisely, and precision is what makes an analysis professional.
:::

## Step 2: Place it on the AI map

Answer two questions, with **evidence** for each claim:

- Is it rule-based AI, machine learning, deep learning, or a **mix**? Which parts are which?
- What is its **narrow** task? (Every system today is narrow AI; your job is to state exactly what its narrow task is.)

Evidence can be: what the company says, what the system does (does it improve over time? does it handle messy inputs like photos or speech?), or what similar systems are known to use. When you are not sure, say so: "probably uses machine learning, because…". Honest uncertainty is better than confident guessing.

## Step 3: Write its PEAS table

| | Your system |
|---|---|
| Performance measure | How is success judged? Include several goals. |
| Environment | Where does it work? What is in that world? |
| Actuators | How does it act? |
| Sensors | How does it perceive? What data does it receive? |

Then name **one risk** that would appear if the performance measure were chosen badly ("what you measure is what you get").

## Step 4: Classify its environment

For each of the five properties, choose a side and give **one sentence of reasoning**:

- Fully or partially observable?
- Deterministic or stochastic?
- Episodic or sequential?
- Static or dynamic?
- Single-agent or multi-agent?

Then say which **type of agent** it most likely is (simple reflex, model-based, goal-based, utility-based, learning).

## Step 5: Name the problem type(s)

Is it classification, regression, clustering, recommendation, anomaly detection, generation or sequential decision-making? Most real systems combine several. List each **feature** of the system with its own problem type.

## Step 6: History and impact

- Which of the three ingredients (**data, computing power, methods**) made this system possible, and when?
- **Who benefits?** Who could be **harmed or left out**? Think about people without smartphones, speakers of other languages, rural areas, people with disabilities, and people whose data is used.

## Model analysis: medical drone delivery in Rwanda

This model shows the level of detail to aim for. It uses only general, publicly described facts about drone delivery; where a detail is not public, it says "probably".

:::example Model analysis, Step 2: the AI map
**System:** autonomous drones that deliver blood and medical supplies from a distribution centre to hospitals and health centres in Rwanda, operating since 2016.

**Place on the AI map: a mix.**
- **Search and planning (AI, not learning):** the system plans flight routes between a centre and many health facilities, which is a search problem over paths in the air, avoiding no-fly areas.
- **Rule-based control (AI and engineering):** safety rules, for example not flying in certain weather, keeping set distances from aircraft and dropping packages only inside marked zones. These are probably written by engineers, not learned.
- **Machine learning (probably):** predicting demand for blood products, or estimating weather risk from past flights, would naturally use models trained on historical data. This is likely, but not confirmed in public sources.

**Narrow task:** deliver urgent medical supplies by air to a known list of facilities, safely and quickly.
:::

:::example Model analysis, Step 3: PEAS
| | Medical drone delivery |
|---|---|
| Performance measure | Time from order to delivery; share of deliveries on time; zero injuries or accidents; package condition (blood kept at the right temperature); cost per delivery; battery use |
| Environment | Rwanda's hills and valleys, wind and rain, airspace shared with aircraft, distribution centre, hospital drop zones, people on the ground |
| Actuators | Motors and flight-control surfaces, package-release mechanism with parachute, radio messages to operators and hospitals |
| Sensors | GPS, airspeed and wind sensors, battery and temperature monitors, possibly cameras; order information from hospitals |

**Risk of a bad performance measure:** if the team rewarded only **speed**, the system might accept flights in risky weather, or skip safety checks. Speed must be balanced with safety and package condition.
:::

:::example Model analysis, Step 4: environment and agent type
- **Partially observable:** the drone cannot know the exact wind ahead or whether a person has walked into the drop zone.
- **Stochastic:** weather and wind change unpredictably.
- **Sequential:** each decision (route, height, when to release) affects what comes next and the battery left.
- **Dynamic:** weather and air traffic change during a flight.
- **Multi-agent:** other aircraft, other drones and people on the ground share the environment.

**Agent type:** a **utility-based agent** (it balances speed, safety, battery and cost), with **learning** components likely used for planning and forecasting.
:::

:::example Model analysis, Step 5: problem types
| Feature | Problem type |
|---|---|
| Planning the flight path | Search and sequential decision-making |
| Keeping the drone stable in wind | Sequential decision-making (control) |
| Predicting which hospitals will need which products | Regression (how many units) |
| Deciding whether weather is safe to fly | Classification (fly / do not fly), possibly with rules |
| Spotting unusual drone behaviour from sensor data | Anomaly detection |
:::

:::example Model analysis, Step 6: history and impact
**Ingredients:** cheap, light **GPS and sensors** from the smartphone industry; better **batteries and motors**; mature **flight-control and route-planning methods**; and **data** from many flights to improve operations. Mobile networks made ordering by phone or SMS possible.

**Who benefits:** patients in rural areas, especially mothers who lose blood after giving birth, and hospitals that cannot store every blood type.

**Who could be harmed or left out:** facilities outside the service area; people on the ground if a release goes wrong; staff whose transport jobs change. Privacy questions arise if drones carry cameras over homes.

**Source:** company website and news reports on drone delivery in Rwanda (name the specific pages you used).
:::

Notice what makes the model strong: every claim is specific, each section uses the correct Week 1 term, uncertainty is stated honestly, and risks are concrete rather than general.

:::mistake Common mistakes in this project
- Writing "it uses AI" without saying **which parts** are rules, search or learning.
- A performance measure with only one goal ("fast").
- Saying "fully observable" for real-world systems, which are almost always partially observable.
- Listing only benefits. A professional analysis always asks who could be harmed or left out.
- No sources.
:::

## Checklist before you submit

- Every section (Steps 2–6) is answered.
- At least **one source** is named (website, article or video).
- You used the correct terms: agent, PEAS, environment properties, agent type, problem type.
- Uncertain claims are marked as uncertain.
- Simple, clear sentences: a manager should understand it in five minutes.

## Summary

- A professional AI analysis places the system on the AI map, writes its PEAS table, classifies its environment, names its problem types and explains its history and impact.
- Real systems are usually a **mix** of rules, search and learning; say which part is which, with evidence.
- Be **specific** and **honest about uncertainty**. Always ask who benefits and who could be harmed or left out.

## Key terms

- **Model analysis** — a worked example of an analysis, showing the expected level of detail.
- **Evidence** — facts that support a claim, such as company documents, observed behaviour or reliable articles.

## Think deeper

1. In the model analysis, the weather decision is described as classification "possibly with rules". Why would a safety-critical system combine a learned model with hard rules?
:::answer
A learned model can weigh many weather factors and spot risky patterns that people might miss, but it can make mistakes on situations it has rarely seen. Hard rules give guaranteed limits that never move, for example "never fly above a certain wind speed", which regulators and engineers can check. Combining both gives the flexibility of learning with the certainty of rules where safety demands it.
:::
2. Choose a recommendation feed such as YouTube. Write one bad and one better performance measure, and the risk of the bad one.
:::answer
Bad: "total minutes watched". The system may push extreme, shocking or addictive videos because they hold attention, even when they harm viewers. Better: a combination such as "viewers report satisfaction with what they watched, return over weeks, and harmful content is rarely shown", balanced with watch time. The better measure tries to capture real value to the viewer, not just attention.
:::

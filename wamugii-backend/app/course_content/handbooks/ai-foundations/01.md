Open your phone. Unlock it with your face. Check a route on Google Maps. Watch a video that YouTube chose for you. Send money with MoMo, where a hidden system checks every transfer for fraud. In five minutes you have used artificial intelligence (AI) four or five times, and you probably did not notice.

Because AI is everywhere, the word is used loosely. Adverts call almost any product "AI-powered". Newspapers say "AI thinks" or "AI wants". In this chapter you will learn to use the word the way professionals do: precisely. That one skill already puts you ahead of most people who talk about AI.

## A precise definition

Most university textbooks agree on a definition close to this one:

> **Artificial intelligence** is the study and building of systems that **perceive** their surroundings, **reason** about what they perceive, and **act** to reach a goal, doing tasks that would normally need human intelligence.

Let us read it slowly, word by word.

- **Perceive** means take in information. A camera sees a face. A fraud system receives the details of a transaction: amount, time, place, phone. A chatbot receives the message you typed.
- **Reason** means work something out from that information. Is this the owner's face? Is this transfer unusual for this customer? What is the user asking for?
- **Act** means do something. Unlock the phone. Block the transfer and send an SMS. Write a reply.
- **To reach a goal** means the system is judged by results. A face-unlock system is good if it lets the owner in quickly and keeps strangers out.

:::key Key idea
A system is intelligent because it **reaches goals well**, not because it copies the human brain.
:::

Notice what the definition does **not** say. It does not say "a computer that thinks like a human" or "a computer with feelings". An aeroplane flies very well, yet it does not flap its wings like a bird. In the same way, an AI system can do an intelligent task well using methods that look nothing like human thinking. A chess program checks millions of possible moves every second. No human plays chess like that, but the program still plays brilliantly.

This matters because many wrong ideas about AI come from imagining it as an electronic person. When you hear "the AI decided", translate it in your head to "the system produced an output, based on its programming and its data". This keeps your thinking clear.

### The "would normally need human intelligence" part

The last part of the definition is about the **task**, not the machine. Adding two numbers is not called AI today, even though it once needed a clever person. Recognising a face, understanding a sentence, planning a route through traffic or diagnosing a disease from an X-ray are tasks we still connect with intelligence, so systems that do them are called AI.

An interesting effect follows: when a task becomes routine for computers, people often stop calling it AI. Spell-checkers and route planners were once research projects in AI laboratories; today most people just call them "features". Researchers sometimes joke that "AI is whatever has not been done yet".

## Three circles inside each other

The three words you will hear most are **artificial intelligence**, **machine learning** and **deep learning**. They are not three separate fields. Picture three circles, one inside the other.

[[fig:circles|AI is the largest circle. Machine learning is a part of AI, and deep learning is a part of machine learning.]]

| Circle | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Artificial intelligence** (biggest) | Any technique that makes machines act intelligently, including hand-written rules and search | A chess program that searches millions of moves |
| **Machine learning** (inside AI) | Systems that **learn patterns from data** instead of following only hand-written rules | A model that predicts loan risk from thousands of past loans |
| **Deep learning** (inside machine learning) | Machine learning with **many-layered neural networks**, trained on large amounts of data | Face unlock on your phone, ChatGPT, Google Translate |

So **every** deep-learning system is a machine-learning system, and **every** machine-learning system is an AI system. But it does not work the other way round: many AI systems do not learn from data at all.

### What does "learning from data" really mean?

Imagine you want a program that decides whether an SMS message is a scam.

**Approach 1, write the rules yourself.** You sit down and write: *if the message contains "you have won" and asks you to send money, mark it as a scam*. You add more rules over time. This is AI (the program acts intelligently), but it is **not** machine learning, because a person wrote every rule.

**Approach 2, learn from examples.** You collect 50,000 old messages that people have already marked as "scam" or "normal". You give them to a learning algorithm. The algorithm finds the patterns by itself: certain words, many capital letters, unknown numbers, links, requests for PINs. The result is a **model** that can judge new messages. This is machine learning.

The big advantage of approach 2 is that it finds patterns people would never think to write down, and it can be updated simply by giving it new examples. The big disadvantage is that it needs a lot of good, correctly labelled data, and it is harder to explain why it made a particular decision.

:::example Worked example: placing products in the right circle
**Google Maps finding the fastest route.** The core is a **search** method that explores roads to find the cheapest path (you will study this in Chapter 5). That is AI but not learning. However, the travel times it uses come from a **machine-learning** model that predicts traffic from millions of phones' past movements. So the product is a **mix**: search (AI) plus machine learning.

**Face unlock.** The phone uses a neural network with many layers, trained on very many face images, to turn your face into a set of numbers and compare it with the stored one. That is **deep learning**.

**A calculator app.** It follows fixed arithmetic. No perceiving, no reasoning about an uncertain world, no learning. It is **not AI**.

**A bank rule "block any transfer above the daily limit".** A hand-written rule. If it is part of a bigger fraud system, it is **rule-based AI**. On its own it is just a business rule.
:::

## Two big families of AI

Since the 1950s, researchers have built intelligent systems in two broad ways.

### Symbolic AI (rule-based)

In **symbolic AI**, people write knowledge down as symbols, rules and logic, for example:

> IF the patient has a fever AND a rash THEN check for measles.

A large collection of such rules, written with the help of experts, was called an **expert system**. These systems were popular in business in the 1980s.

- **Strengths:** the logic is clear and easy to check; it works well when the rules of a situation are known and stable (tax rules, eligibility rules, games with fixed rules).
- **Weaknesses:** the real world is messy. Nobody can write rules for every way a cat can look in a photo, or every way a person can phrase a question. Rule collections also become very hard to maintain as they grow.

### Statistical AI (learning-based)

In **statistical AI**, the system learns from examples. Machine learning and deep learning belong here.

- **Strengths:** handles messy data such as photos, speech and everyday language; improves when given more data; finds patterns people miss.
- **Weaknesses:** needs lots of good data; can learn unfair or wrong patterns hidden in the data; is often hard to explain.

### Most real systems mix both

Modern products mostly rely on learning, but very many still combine both families. A mobile-money fraud system may use a learned model that gives each transfer a "risk score", **and** hard rules such as "always ask for a PIN above 500,000 RWF" or "never allow transfers to a blocked number". The rules give certainty where the law or the business demands it; the model catches clever new patterns.

:::mistake Common mistake
Thinking that "real AI" means only machine learning or chatbots. Search, planning and rule-based systems are AI too. When you analyse a product, always ask: **which parts are rules, which parts are search, and which parts are learned?**
:::

## Narrow AI and general AI

### Narrow AI

**Narrow AI** (also called "weak AI") does one kind of task, or a small family of tasks: translating text, recognising faces, recommending videos, playing chess, detecting disease in X-rays.

**All AI that exists today is narrow.** That includes very capable chatbots. A large language model can write, summarise, translate and answer questions across many topics, which is impressive breadth. But it still works in one way (predicting text), it can fail at simple tasks outside its training, it has no goals of its own in the world, and it cannot, for example, learn to ride a bicycle or run a farm by itself.

### Artificial general intelligence

**Artificial general intelligence (AGI)** would match or beat people across almost all thinking tasks, and could learn new kinds of tasks the way a person can. It is a research goal and a subject of serious debate:

- Some researchers believe it could arrive within decades and want careful preparation for its risks.
- Others believe it is much further away, or that the idea is not well defined.

What everybody agrees on: AGI is **not a product you can buy today**.

:::think Think about it
A headline says: "AI now reads X-rays better than doctors". What questions would you ask before believing it? (Which disease? Which hospitals' X-rays? Compared with which doctors, under which conditions? Was it tested on new patients?) Most such headlines describe **one narrow system** doing **one task** under **certain conditions**.
:::

## The basic vocabulary

You will meet these words in every chapter. Learn them well now.

- **Agent**: anything that perceives its environment and acts on it. A robot, a chatbot and a person are all agents. You will study agents in Chapter 3.
- **Algorithm**: a step-by-step method for solving a problem, like a recipe. A **learning algorithm** is a recipe for finding patterns in data.
- **Model**: the result of learning, the stored pattern that the algorithm found. If the algorithm is the recipe, the model is the cake.
- **Training**: the process of running a learning algorithm on data to produce a model.
- **Inference**: using a finished, trained model to make a prediction on new input. Unlocking your phone is inference; the training happened long before, in a laboratory.
- **Data**: the examples a learning system learns from (Chapter 7).

:::example Worked example: one product, all the words
A crop-disease app lets a farmer photograph a cassava leaf and tells her whether the plant is healthy.

- The **data** was thousands of leaf photos, each marked by experts as healthy or diseased.
- A **learning algorithm** (here, training a neural network) was run on that data. That process was **training**.
- The result is a **model** stored inside the app.
- When the farmer takes a new photo and gets an answer, the app is doing **inference**.
- The whole app, which takes in a photo (perceives), works out the likely disease (reasons) and shows advice (acts), is an **agent**.
- Since it uses a many-layered neural network, it belongs to the **deep learning** circle.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Look around Kigali and you will find all three circles. Irembo uses rules to decide which documents a service needs (rule-based logic). Banks and mobile-money operators score transactions for fraud risk (machine learning). Translation and speech tools for Kinyarwanda are built with deep learning, and they are improving as more Kinyarwanda data becomes available.
:::

## Summary

- AI is the study and building of systems that **perceive, reason and act** to reach goals that normally need human intelligence. It does not need to copy the human brain.
- **AI ⊃ machine learning ⊃ deep learning.** Machine learning learns patterns from data; deep learning does it with many-layered neural networks.
- **Symbolic AI** uses rules written by people; **statistical AI** learns from examples. Most real products mix both.
- **All AI today is narrow.** AGI is a research goal, not a product.
- An **algorithm** is the method, a **model** is what it learns, **training** builds the model and **inference** uses it.

## Key terms

- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** — systems that perceive, reason and act to reach goals that normally need human intelligence.
- **Machine learning (ML)** — the part of AI where systems learn patterns from data instead of following only hand-written rules.
- **Deep learning** — machine learning using neural networks with many layers.
- **Symbolic AI** — AI built from rules and logic written by people.
- **Expert system** — a program that stores experts' knowledge as many rules.
- **Narrow AI** — AI that does one task or a small family of tasks; all AI today is narrow.
- **Artificial general intelligence (AGI)** — hypothetical AI that matches people across almost all thinking tasks.
- **Algorithm** — a step-by-step method for solving a problem.
- **Model** — the pattern a learning algorithm finds and stores.
- **Training** — running a learning algorithm on data to produce a model.
- **Inference** — using a trained model to make predictions on new input.

## Think deeper

1. Your friend says: "A calculator is AI because it is smarter than me at maths." Use the definition from this chapter to explain whether they are right.
:::answer
They are not right. Being better than a person at one calculation is not the test. The definition asks whether the system perceives its surroundings, reasons about uncertain information and acts to reach a goal in a task that needs intelligence. A calculator follows fixed arithmetic steps on exact input; nothing is uncertain, it perceives nothing about the world and it makes no choices. It is a useful tool, but it is not AI.
:::
2. Explain, using one example, why every deep-learning system is a machine-learning system but not every AI system is a machine-learning system.
:::answer
Face unlock uses a neural network with many layers that learned from many face images. Because it learned from data, it is machine learning, and because it uses a deep network, it is deep learning. But a route-planning search program, or a rule-based eligibility checker, acts intelligently without learning anything from data. These are AI but sit outside the machine-learning circle. Deep learning is inside machine learning, which is inside AI, so the containment only works in one direction.
:::
3. A company says its new chatbot "has general intelligence because it can answer questions on any topic". Give two reasons to doubt this claim.
:::answer
First, answering questions in text is still one kind of task: predicting text. General intelligence would mean learning and doing almost any thinking task, including ones far from text, such as planning and running a business in the real world. Second, such chatbots still make simple mistakes outside their training, can state false information confidently and cannot learn new skills by themselves the way a person can. So it is a broad but still narrow system.
:::

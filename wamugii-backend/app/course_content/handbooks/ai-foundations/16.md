In Chapter 15 you saw that a neural network is a huge collection of weights and biases. A new network starts with **random** values, so its first predictions are nonsense: it might say a healthy leaf is 33% healthy, 33% mosaic and 33% brown streak, for every photo.

Training turns that random network into a useful one by adjusting millions of weights, **little by little**, so the predictions become less wrong. Four ideas make this possible: the **loss**, **gradient descent**, the **learning rate** and **backpropagation**. This chapter explains each one with numbers you can check yourself.

## Idea 1: the loss function, measuring the mistake

The **loss** is a single number that says **how wrong** the network is on a set of examples. Small loss means good predictions; big loss means bad ones. The aim of training is easy to state: **make the loss as small as possible.**

### Mean squared error (for numbers)

For regression, a common loss is **mean squared error (MSE)**: the average of the squared differences between predictions and true values.

:::example Worked example: MSE
A network predicts house prices (millions RWF) for three houses:
- True 30, predicted 28: error −2, squared 4.
- True 45, predicted 50: error +5, squared 25.
- True 20, predicted 20: error 0, squared 0.

MSE = (4 + 25 + 0) ÷ 3 = 29 ÷ 3 ≈ **9.7**. Squaring makes all errors positive and punishes big errors heavily: the 5-million error contributes six times as much as the 2-million error.
:::

### Cross-entropy (for categories)

For classification, the common loss is **cross-entropy**. It looks at the probability the network gave to the **correct** class, and punishes it when that probability is low, especially when the network was confidently wrong.

| Probability given to the correct class | Cross-entropy loss (−ln p) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0.9 | 0.11 | Confident and right: small loss |
| 0.5 | 0.69 | Unsure: medium loss |
| 0.1 | 2.30 | Confidently wrong: big loss |
| 0.01 | 4.61 | Very confidently wrong: very big loss |

You do not need to calculate logarithms yourself. The pattern is what matters: **confident wrong answers cost the most**, which pushes the network to be both correct and honest about uncertainty.

## Idea 2: gradient descent, walking downhill

Imagine you are on a hill near Nyungwe forest in thick fog, and you want to reach the bottom of the valley. You cannot see more than a metre. But you can **feel the slope** under your feet. So you take a step in the direction that goes downhill most steeply, feel again, take another step, and repeat. Eventually you reach the bottom.

That is **gradient descent**:

- The **hill** is the loss. Every possible setting of the weights is a position on the hill; its height is the loss at that setting.
- The **gradient** is the slope: for each weight, it says which direction makes the loss **rise** fastest, and how steeply.
- We step in the **opposite** direction of the gradient, so the loss goes **down**.
- Each step changes every weight a little: **new weight = old weight − learning rate × gradient**.

[[fig:descent|Gradient descent on a simple loss, (w − 3)², starting at w = 0. Each step moves downhill, taking smaller steps as the slope flattens near the bottom at w = 3.]]

:::example Worked example: gradient descent by hand
Take a network with just **one** weight, w, and suppose its loss is **(w − 3)²**. The best value is obviously w = 3 (loss 0), but let us see how gradient descent finds it without knowing that.

For this loss, the gradient (the slope) at any point is **2 × (w − 3)**. Start at **w = 0** with **learning rate 0.1**.

| Step | w | Loss (w − 3)² | Gradient 2(w − 3) | New w = w − 0.1 × gradient |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.000 | 9.00 | −6.00 | 0 + 0.60 = 0.600 |
| 1 | 0.600 | 5.76 | −4.80 | 0.600 + 0.48 = 1.080 |
| 2 | 1.080 | 3.69 | −3.84 | 1.080 + 0.384 = 1.464 |
| 3 | 1.464 | 2.36 | −3.07 | 1.464 + 0.307 = 1.771 |
| 4 | 1.771 | 1.51 | −2.46 | 1.771 + 0.246 = 2.017 |

The loss falls every step: 9.00 → 5.76 → 3.69 → 2.36 → 1.51, and w moves steadily towards 3. Notice the steps get **smaller** as the slope flattens near the bottom. That happens automatically.
:::

A real network does exactly this, but for **millions of weights at the same time**: each weight gets its own gradient and its own small step.

## Idea 3: the learning rate, choosing the step size

The **learning rate** decides how big each step is. It is one of the most important settings in deep learning.

| Learning rate | What happens |
|---|---|
| Too small | Progress is very slow; training takes forever |
| Too large | You jump over the valley and bounce around, or even climb higher |
| Just right | Steady progress down to a low loss |

:::example Worked example: a learning rate that is too large
Same loss, (w − 3)², start at w = 0, but now learning rate **1.1**.

- Step 0: gradient −6, new w = 0 + 6.6 = **6.6** (loss 12.96, worse than the start's 9.00).
- Step 1: gradient 2 × 3.6 = 7.2, new w = 6.6 − 7.92 = **−1.32** (loss 18.66).
- Step 2: gradient 2 × (−4.32) = −8.64, new w = −1.32 + 9.50 = **8.18** (loss 26.87).

Each step jumps right over the valley and lands higher on the other side. The loss **grows** instead of shrinking: training has **diverged**.

With learning rate **0.01**, the first step only moves w from 0 to 0.06 (loss 8.64). It is safe, but it would take hundreds of steps to get close to 3.
:::

Settings like the learning rate, the number of layers and the number of epochs are chosen by people, not learned from data. They are called **hyperparameters**, and teams choose them by trying values and comparing results on the **validation set** (Chapter 13).

## Idea 4: backpropagation, sharing the blame

A deep network has many layers. When the final output is wrong, **which** of the millions of weights are to blame, and by how much? **Backpropagation** answers this question.

Think of a relay team that loses a race. The coach works **backwards** from the finish: the last runner lost 2 seconds; the third runner handed over late, costing 1 second; the second runner was fine. Each runner gets feedback in proportion to their part in the loss.

Backpropagation does the same. Starting at the output, it works **backwards** layer by layer, calculating for every weight how much a small change in that weight would change the loss. That is exactly the **gradient** that gradient descent needs. Then each weight is adjusted by its own share of the blame.

The mathematics is calculus (the chain rule), but the computer does it automatically. Backpropagation became popular in the **1980s** and is still how almost every neural network is trained today, including the largest language models.

:::key Key idea
**Backpropagation** works out the gradient for every weight. **Gradient descent** uses those gradients to update the weights. Together they are how neural networks learn.
:::

## The training loop

Putting the four ideas together, training repeats this loop:

1. Take a small group of training examples, called a **batch** (for example, 32 or 100 examples).
2. **Forward pass:** the network makes predictions for the batch.
3. Calculate the **loss**: how wrong were those predictions?
4. **Backward pass:** backpropagation computes the gradient for every weight.
5. **Update:** gradient descent changes each weight a little.
6. Move to the next batch. One full pass through all the training data is an **epoch**. Training usually runs for many epochs.

Why batches? Using the whole dataset for every step would be slow, and using one example at a time would be noisy. Batches are a practical middle way.

:::example Worked example: counting updates
A dataset has 10,000 training examples and the batch size is 100.
- Updates per epoch = 10,000 ÷ 100 = **100**.
- Training for 20 epochs = 100 × 20 = **2,000** weight updates.
:::

## Reading the loss curves

During training, teams plot two lines: the **training loss** (on the training data) and the **validation loss** (on data held aside, Chapter 13).

| What you see | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Both losses fall together and level off | Healthy learning | Keep the model from the point where validation loss is lowest |
| Training loss keeps falling, validation loss starts **rising** | **Overfitting**: the network is memorising | Stop early (**early stopping**), add regularisation or dropout, get more data |
| Both losses stay high | **Underfitting**: the model is too simple or training too short | Bigger model, better features, train longer |
| Loss jumps up and down wildly, or grows | Learning rate probably **too large** | Lower the learning rate |
| Loss falls extremely slowly | Learning rate possibly **too small** | Raise the learning rate carefully |

:::mistake Common mistake
Watching only the training loss and stopping when it is lowest. A very low training loss can simply mean the network has memorised its training data. The **validation** loss tells you when to stop.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Training large networks needs GPUs and electricity, which can be expensive. A practical approach for teams here is to start from a model already trained on huge data elsewhere, and **fine-tune** it on a smaller local dataset (for example, Rwandan crop photos or Kinyarwanda text). Fine-tuning uses exactly the same loop described in this chapter, but needs far fewer examples and much less computing.
:::

## Summary

- The **loss** measures how wrong the network is: **MSE** for numbers, **cross-entropy** for categories (which punishes confident wrong answers most).
- **Gradient descent** repeatedly steps opposite the gradient: new weight = old weight − learning rate × gradient.
- The **learning rate** sets the step size: too small is slow, too large overshoots and can diverge.
- **Backpropagation** works backwards through the layers to compute every weight's gradient, its share of the blame.
- Training loops over **batches** and **epochs**. Watch the **validation loss** to catch overfitting and know when to stop.

## Key terms

- **Loss function** — a formula giving one number for how wrong the predictions are.
- **Mean squared error (MSE)** — the average of squared differences between predictions and true values.
- **Cross-entropy** — a classification loss that punishes low probability on the correct class.
- **Gradient** — the direction and steepness in which the loss rises for each weight.
- **Gradient descent** — repeatedly adjusting weights in the direction that lowers the loss.
- **Learning rate** — the size of each gradient-descent step.
- **Diverge** — when training becomes unstable and the loss grows instead of shrinking.
- **Backpropagation** — computing each weight's gradient by working backwards from the output.
- **Forward pass** — running inputs through the network to get predictions.
- **Backward pass** — running backpropagation to get gradients.
- **Batch** — a small group of examples used for one weight update.
- **Epoch** — one full pass through all the training data.
- **Hyperparameter** — a setting chosen by people rather than learned, such as the learning rate.
- **Fine-tuning** — continuing to train an already-trained model on new, usually smaller, data.

## Think deeper

1. Continue the first worked example (learning rate 0.1) for one more step from w = 2.017. What are the new w and the new loss?
:::answer
The gradient is 2 × (2.017 − 3) = 2 × (−0.983) = −1.966. New w = 2.017 − 0.1 × (−1.966) = 2.017 + 0.197 = 2.214. New loss = (2.214 − 3)² = (−0.786)² ≈ 0.62. The loss keeps falling, from 0.97 at w = 2.017 to about 0.62, and the step is smaller again because the slope is flatter.
:::
2. A team's training loss falls quickly, then jumps up and down wildly. What would you change first, and why?
:::answer
Lower the learning rate. Wild jumps suggest the steps are so big that they overshoot the bottom of the valley and bounce from side to side. Smaller steps let training settle into a low loss. If the loss starts to fall smoothly again, the learning rate was the problem.
:::
3. Why does cross-entropy give a bigger loss for a confident wrong answer (probability 0.01 on the correct class) than for an unsure wrong answer (probability 0.4)?
:::answer
Cross-entropy is designed to punish overconfidence. Saying "almost certainly not the right class" when it is the right class is a worse mistake than saying "I'm unsure". The loss for 0.01 is about 4.6, while for 0.4 it is about 0.9. This pushes the network not only to be right, but to be honest about how sure it is, which matters when people rely on its probabilities.
:::

Walk into Kimironko market for the first time. Nobody gives you a map, yet within minutes you notice that the stalls naturally form groups: vegetables in one area, fabrics in another, phones and electronics somewhere else. You discovered that structure **without being told the answer**.

That is the idea of **unsupervised learning**. The data has **no labels**. Nobody tells the computer the right answer. Instead, it looks for **structure**: groups, patterns, and points that do not fit.

## When is unsupervised learning useful?

- When **labels are expensive or impossible** to get. Labelling millions of customers by hand is not practical.
- When you **don't know what you are looking for**: you want to **discover** groups or patterns nobody has named yet.
- When the interesting events are **rare and new**, such as a never-seen-before fraud trick.
- As a **first step** before supervised learning, to understand and simplify the data.

The trade-off: because there is no answer key, judging an unsupervised result is harder. **People** must decide whether the patterns found are real, sensible and useful.

## Clustering

**Clustering** puts similar examples into groups called **clusters**. Examples in the same cluster should be similar to each other and different from examples in other clusters.

### The k-means algorithm

**k-means** is the most popular clustering method. It works in a simple loop:

1. **Choose k**, the number of groups you want.
2. **Place k starting centres** (often at randomly chosen examples).
3. **Assign** every example to its **nearest** centre.
4. **Move** each centre to the **average** (middle) of the examples assigned to it.
5. **Repeat** steps 3 and 4 until the assignments stop changing.

:::example Worked example: k-means by hand
Six small shops record their average daily customers: **1, 2, 3, 10, 11, 12** (in tens of customers). We want **k = 2** groups. Suppose the starting centres are **1** and **2**.

**Round 1, assign:** each shop goes to its nearest centre.
- Shop at 1 → nearest centre is 1.
- Shops at 2, 3, 10, 11, 12 → nearest centre is 2.

**Round 1, move:** centre 1 = average of {1} = **1**. Centre 2 = average of {2, 3, 10, 11, 12} = 38 ÷ 5 = **7.6**.

**Round 2, assign:**
- Shop at 2: distance to 1 is 1, to 7.6 is 5.6 → centre 1.
- Shop at 3: distance to 1 is 2, to 7.6 is 4.6 → centre 1.
- Shops at 10, 11, 12 → centre 7.6.

**Round 2, move:** centre 1 = average of {1, 2, 3} = **2**. Centre 2 = average of {10, 11, 12} = **11**.

**Round 3, assign:** nothing changes, so the algorithm stops.

Result: two clusters, **small shops (1, 2, 3)** and **busy shops (10, 11, 12)**. A bad start (both centres at the low end) was corrected automatically by the loop.
:::

Real data has many features, not one, but the steps are identical: "nearest" is measured as distance across all features, and the centre is the average of each feature. As with k-NN (Chapter 9), features should be **scaled** first so that one large-valued feature does not dominate.

### From clusters to decisions

A telecom company clusters customers by calls, data use and mobile-money activity. It may discover:

| Cluster | Pattern | Possible offer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Heavy data, few calls (often students) | Night data bundles |
| 2 | Many calls, little data (often traders) | Cheap call minutes |
| 3 | High mobile-money use (often shop owners) | Merchant services |

Nobody labelled these groups: the data revealed them. But the **names** ("students", "traders") and the **offers** come from **people** who interpret the clusters. That part still needs human judgement, and it can be wrong, so teams check their interpretation, for example by talking to customers in each cluster.

### Choosing k

There is no single correct k. Teams try several values and ask:

- Are the groups **clearly separated**, or do they overlap a lot?
- Is each group **big enough** to matter?
- Can we **act** on the groups? Three clusters with clear offers beat ten clusters nobody can explain.

A common technique is to plot how tight the clusters are for k = 2, 3, 4, … and look for the point where adding another cluster stops helping much (often called the **elbow**).

:::mistake Common mistake
Believing clusters are "real categories that exist in nature". k-means will **always** produce exactly k groups, even if the data has no natural groups at all. Clusters are a useful **summary**, which you must check, not a discovered truth.
:::

## Anomaly detection

**Anomaly detection** learns what **normal** looks like and flags what is **far from normal**: a login from an unusual country at 3 a.m., a machine sensor reading that suddenly jumps, a mobile-money transfer far larger than the customer's usual pattern, a sudden spike in a clinic's malaria cases.

It is especially useful when unusual events are **rare** or **new**, so there are too few labelled examples to train a classifier (Chapter 4). The flagged cases usually go to a **person** to check: an anomaly is "unusual", not automatically "bad".

## Dimensionality reduction

Real datasets can have hundreds of features. **Dimensionality reduction** squeezes many features into a **few new ones** while keeping most of the information. A common method is **PCA (principal component analysis)**, which finds the directions in which the data varies most.

Why it helps:

- **Visualisation:** 50 features can be squeezed into 2 so the data can be drawn on a chart and inspected by eye.
- **Speed:** other models train faster on fewer features.
- **Noise reduction:** small, unimportant variations are dropped.

Example: a survey has 40 questions about a household's assets and spending. Many questions move together (households with electricity often have a fridge and a TV). PCA can combine them into a few summary scores, such as an overall "living standard" score.

## Association rules

**Association rules** find items that often appear **together**: "people who buy bread often buy milk". Shops use them for shelf layout, bundles and offers; streaming services use similar ideas for "people who watched this also watched".

:::example Worked example: is "bread → milk" a strong rule?
A supermarket looks at **1,000** shopping baskets:
- **300** contain bread.
- **400** contain milk.
- **180** contain both bread and milk.

**Support** (how common the pair is) = 180 ÷ 1,000 = **18%** of baskets.

**Confidence** (when someone buys bread, how often do they also buy milk?) = 180 ÷ 300 = **60%**.

**Lift** (is this more than chance?) = confidence ÷ (share of all baskets with milk) = 0.60 ÷ 0.40 = **1.5**. A lift above 1 means bread buyers buy milk **more often than shoppers in general**, so the rule is meaningful.
:::

## Supervised vs. unsupervised

| | Supervised | Unsupervised |
|---|---|---|
| Labels? | Yes | No |
| Goal | Predict a known kind of answer | Discover structure |
| Typical tasks | Classification, regression | Clustering, anomaly detection, dimensionality reduction, association rules |
| How to judge | Compare predictions with true labels | Usefulness and clarity, judged by people |

### Two learning styles in between

- **Semi-supervised learning** uses **a few labelled** examples plus **many unlabelled** ones. Example: 500 leaf photos labelled by agronomists and 50,000 unlabelled photos from farmers.
- **Self-supervised learning** makes the data **create its own labels**. For example, hide a word in a sentence and train the model to guess it: the hidden word is the label, and no person had to write it. This is how **large language models are pre-trained** on huge amounts of text (Chapter 17).

:::rwanda In Rwanda
A savings group federation could cluster thousands of groups by savings, loan sizes and repayment patterns to design different training programmes for each type. A utility company could use anomaly detection on smart-meter readings to find possible faults or illegal connections, sending only the flagged cases to technicians. In both cases the algorithm finds patterns, and local staff decide what they mean.
:::

## Summary

- **Unsupervised learning** finds structure in data **without labels**.
- **k-means** repeats two steps, assign each example to its nearest centre and move centres to the average, until nothing changes.
- **Choosing k** is a judgement: clear, sizeable, **actionable** groups. People interpret and name clusters.
- **Anomaly detection** flags what is far from normal; **dimensionality reduction** (such as PCA) squeezes many features into a few; **association rules** find items that occur together.
- **Semi-supervised** learning mixes few labels with much unlabelled data; **self-supervised** learning creates labels from the data itself.

## Key terms

- **Unsupervised learning** — learning from data without labels to find structure.
- **Clustering** — grouping similar examples together.
- **Cluster** — one group found by clustering.
- **k-means** — a clustering method that alternates assigning examples to the nearest centre and moving centres to the average.
- **Centroid** — the centre (average point) of a cluster.
- **Elbow method** — choosing k at the point where more clusters stop helping much.
- **Dimensionality reduction** — squeezing many features into fewer while keeping most information.
- **PCA (principal component analysis)** — a common dimensionality-reduction method.
- **Association rule** — a pattern of items that often appear together, such as bread and milk.
- **Semi-supervised learning** — learning from a few labelled and many unlabelled examples.
- **Self-supervised learning** — learning where the data supplies its own labels.

## Think deeper

1. Run k-means on the numbers 2, 4, 6, 20, 22, 24 with k = 2, starting centres 2 and 4. Show each round.
:::answer
Round 1 assign: 2 → centre 2; 4, 6, 20, 22, 24 → centre 4. Move: centre A = 2; centre B = (4 + 6 + 20 + 22 + 24) ÷ 5 = 76 ÷ 5 = 15.2. Round 2 assign: 2, 4, 6 → centre 2 (for 6: distance 4 to centre A against 9.2 to centre B); 20, 22, 24 → centre 15.2. Move: centre A = (2 + 4 + 6) ÷ 3 = 4; centre B = (20 + 22 + 24) ÷ 3 = 22. Round 3: nothing changes, so stop. Clusters: {2, 4, 6} and {20, 22, 24}.
:::
2. A shop finds the rule "phone credit → bread" with confidence 50%. 50% of all baskets contain bread. Is the rule useful? Explain using lift.
:::answer
Lift = confidence ÷ share of baskets with bread = 0.50 ÷ 0.50 = 1.0. A lift of 1 means people who buy phone credit buy bread exactly as often as everyone else, so there is no real association. The rule looks impressive at 50% only because bread is popular in general; it is not useful for decisions.
:::
3. Why do flagged anomalies usually go to a person instead of being blocked automatically?
:::answer
An anomaly is only unusual, not necessarily wrong. A large transfer could be fraud, or it could be a customer paying school fees for the year. Automatically blocking every anomaly would harm many honest people. A person can check the context, contact the customer and decide, while the system helps by narrowing down which cases deserve attention.
:::

This is the final project of AI Foundations. You will choose one real problem in Rwanda or your community that AI could help with, and write an **AI Problem Analysis** of **4–6 pages** (or 12–15 slides).

This is the kind of document a consultant, analyst or founder writes **before** any money is spent on building. It answers: *Is this a good AI problem? What would we build, with which data? How would we know it works? Is it safe, fair and lawful? Should we go ahead?* It is also an excellent first item for your portfolio (Chapter 23).

You have already done much of the thinking in your Week 1, 2 and 3 projects. Now you bring it together. A condensed **model analysis** at the end of the chapter shows how the parts connect.

## Part 1: The problem (Week 1)

- **Who** has the problem, **how big** is it, and how is it handled **today**?
- Describe the AI system as an **agent**: write its **PEAS** table (Chapter 3) and classify its **environment** on the five properties.
- Name the **problem type**: classification, regression, clustering, anomaly detection, recommendation, generation or sequential decision-making (Chapter 4).

## Part 2: The learning plan (Week 2)

- **Learning type:** supervised, unsupervised or reinforcement, and why (Chapters 9–11).
- **Data:** features and label, sources, quality risks, a **leakage** check, and whether the data is **representative** (Chapter 7).
- **Workflow:** a short plan for each of the seven stages, including **deployment** and monitoring for **drift** (Chapter 8).
- **Justify AI:** could a simple rule do the job? Why is AI worth its cost here?

## Part 3: Model and evaluation (Week 3)

- A **baseline** method and a **stronger** method, with the trade-off (Chapters 8–9).
- How you will **split** the data: training, validation and test, and whether by time or place (Chapter 13).
- The **main measure** (accuracy, precision, recall, MAE…) and **why**: which mistake is more costly? (Chapter 14.)
- A numeric **success target**, linked to real capacity where relevant (Chapter 18).

## Part 4: Responsible AI (Week 4)

- **Fairness:** which groups could get worse results, which **per-group checks** you will run, and any **proxy** features (Chapter 19).
- **Explainability:** how will a person understand or challenge a decision? Include an example **counterfactual** (Chapter 20).
- **Privacy and security:** which personal data is used, how you will follow **Law No. 058/2021**, and one security threat with a protection (Chapter 21).
- **Governance:** the **risk level** (using the EU AI Act categories as a guide), **who is accountable**, and how users can **reach a human** (Chapter 22).

## Part 5: Recommendation

One page that is **decisive**:

- Should this project **go ahead**? Yes, no, or "yes, if…"?
- What is the **first small step**, a **pilot**? (For example: one district, one branch, three months, with clear measures.)
- What would make you **stop**? (For example: recall below the target, unfair results for a group, users not acting on the alerts.)

## Model analysis (condensed): warning customers about scam SMS

:::example Model analysis, Parts 1 and 2
**Problem:** customers of a mobile-money service receive scam SMS messages ("You have won 500,000 RWF, send 5,000 RWF to claim"). Many lose money. Today, customers rely on their own judgement and occasional awareness campaigns.

**Agent and PEAS:** a warning system inside the messaging flow.
- **Performance measure:** share of scam messages that receive a warning (recall), share of normal messages wrongly warned (false-positive rate), reduction in reported scam losses.
- **Environment:** SMS traffic in Kinyarwanda, English and French; scammers who change their wording.
- **Actuators:** a warning label on the message; a tip on how to report it.
- **Sensors:** message text, sender type (unknown number or registered business), links.

**Environment:** partially observable (intent is hidden), stochastic, sequential over time, dynamic and **multi-agent**: scammers actively adapt.

**Problem type:** binary **classification** (scam or normal), plus **anomaly detection** to catch new scam styles.

**Learning type and data:** supervised, using messages that customers already reported as scams, plus normal messages shared with consent. Features: words and phrases, presence of links, requests for money or PINs, sender type. **Leakage check:** "message reported by many users" is only known later, so it is excluded from model inputs. **Representativeness:** the data must include Kinyarwanda messages and rural users, not only English messages from Kigali.

**Why AI?** A keyword rule ("won", "prize") is the **baseline**, but scammers quickly change words; a learned model can recognise patterns across many wordings and languages.
:::

:::example Model analysis, Parts 3 and 4
**Methods:** baseline keyword rule; stronger method logistic regression on text features, later possibly a small language model.

**Split:** by **time**: train on older months, test on the most recent month, because scammers change tactics.

**Main measure:** **recall** (catch scams) with a strict limit on false positives, because warning on genuine messages (from a bank or a family member) would make customers ignore all warnings. **Target:** warn on at least 80% of scam messages while wrongly warning on fewer than 1 in 200 normal messages.

**Fairness:** check recall separately for messages in Kinyarwanda, English and French. A model that protects English readers better than Kinyarwanda readers would leave the most vulnerable customers less protected.

**Explainability:** each warning shows a short reason: "This message asks you to send money to claim a prize, a common scam pattern." A counterfactual for internal review: "Without the link and the request for money, this message would not have been flagged."

**Privacy and security:** message content is highly personal. Lawful basis and clear customer notice under **Law No. 058/2021**; process messages automatically without staff reading them; keep only flagged examples, pseudonymised; store data in Rwanda. **Security threat:** scammers test messages to find wording that avoids detection (adversarial examples). Protection: frequent retraining and anomaly detection for new patterns.

**Governance:** limited-risk in spirit (it informs, it does not block), but it affects trust and money, so a **named product owner** is accountable, results are monitored weekly, and customers can report wrong warnings and reach a person.
:::

:::example Model analysis, Part 5: recommendation
**Go ahead, as a pilot.** Run a three-month pilot for 10,000 volunteer customers in two districts, one urban and one rural. Warnings only inform; no messages are blocked.

**Success:** recall at least 80% and false warnings under 1 in 200 in **every** language group; reported scam losses among pilot customers fall compared with similar non-pilot customers.

**Stop or redesign if:** false warnings exceed the limit (customers will learn to ignore them), recall in Kinyarwanda falls far below other languages, or customers report privacy concerns that the notice and design cannot resolve.
:::

Notice how every part **connects**: the performance measure in Part 1 becomes the evaluation target in Part 3; the multi-agent environment explains why the split is by time and why monitoring matters; the representativeness concern in Part 2 becomes the per-language fairness check in Part 4; and the recommendation uses those same numbers as its stopping conditions. That connection is what makes an analysis professional.

## Marking guide (self-check)

| Area | What "excellent" looks like | What "needs work" looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem framing** | Clear user, decision and numeric success measure | "Use AI to improve X" with no decision or number |
| **Technical correctness** | Correct agent description, problem type, learning type and measures | Problem type or measures confused (for example accuracy for a rare event) |
| **Data thinking** | Specific features, honest quality, representativeness and leakage checks | A vague list of "data" with no sources or risks |
| **Responsibility** | Concrete fairness checks per group, privacy steps under the law, accountability | General statements like "we will be fair and ethical" |
| **Communication** | Simple English, clear structure; a busy reader understands quickly | Long, complex sentences; no headings; jargon without explanation |

:::mistake Common mistakes in the final project
- Five separate mini-essays that do not connect to each other.
- A recommendation that says "it depends" without saying on **what**.
- Treating responsible AI as one paragraph at the end instead of a full part with specific checks.
- No pilot: proposing to launch everywhere at once.
:::

- A professional analysis **connects** the parts: the performance measure becomes the evaluation target and the stopping condition.

## Key terms

- **AI problem analysis** — a document assessing whether and how AI should be used for a problem, before building.
- **Pilot** — a small, time-limited trial with clear measures before a full launch.
- **Stopping condition** — a pre-agreed result that would make a team pause or end a project.

## Think deeper

1. In the model analysis, why are messages "only warned, not blocked" during the pilot?
:::answer
Blocking would turn every false positive into real harm: a customer might miss a genuine message from their bank or family. Warnings let the team measure recall and false-positive rates in real conditions while the customer stays in control. If the results are excellent and stable, stronger actions could be considered later, with clear appeal routes.
:::
2. Why is the evaluation split by time in the model analysis?
:::answer
Scammers keep changing their messages, so the model must work on future messages it has never seen. Training on older months and testing on the latest month copies real use and reveals whether the model can keep up with new tactics. A random split would mix new tactics into training and make results look better than they will be in practice.
:::
3. Write one stopping condition for your own final project, and explain why you chose it.
:::answer
Example: "Pause the pilot if recall for rural users is more than 10 points lower than for urban users after two months." This was chosen because the project's main fairness risk is that rural users are under-represented in the data; a gap of this size would mean the system leaves the people with the least access to services less protected, which would defeat its purpose.
:::

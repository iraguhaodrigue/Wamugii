You apply for a loan and receive an SMS: *"Your application has been declined."* You ask why. The officer says: *"The system decided."* How would you feel? You cannot fix the problem, you cannot challenge a mistake, and you cannot even know whether the reason was fair.

As AI systems make or support more decisions about people, the question **"why?"** becomes essential. This chapter explains why some models cannot easily answer it, and the tools professionals use to get useful answers anyway.

## The black-box problem

Some models show their reasoning directly. A small decision tree (Chapter 9) can be read like a flowchart: *sales above 100,000 RWF → fewer than 2 late payments → approve*. A logistic regression shows how much each feature pushes the probability up or down.

A deep neural network with millions of weights (Chapter 15) cannot be read this way. It may be very accurate, but nobody can easily say **why** it made a particular decision, because the "reasoning" is spread across millions of numbers. Such models are called **black boxes**: you can see what goes in and what comes out, but not what happens inside.

## Why explanations matter

| Who | Why they need explanations |
|---|---|
| **The affected person** | To understand the decision, challenge a mistake, or know what to change ("build a longer savings history") |
| **The organisation** | To trust the model, catch errors before they cause harm, and meet legal and ethical duties |
| **Engineers** | To debug the model and find data problems, leakage or shortcuts |
| **Regulators and auditors** | To check that the system is safe, fair and lawful |

Explanations also protect against **hidden bias** (Chapter 19). If a loan model's main reason for rejection turns out to be the applicant's home district, that is a warning that the model may be using a proxy for something unfair.

## Two approaches

1. **Use an interpretable model from the start.** Linear or logistic regression and small decision trees can be read directly. For many tabular problems (rows and columns of numbers), they perform nearly as well as complex models.
2. **Explain a complex model after training.** Use tools that describe what the black box is doing, overall or for one decision.

:::key A good rule
**Start with the simplest model that works.** Use a black box only when it is **clearly** better on validation data, and then add explanation tools. For high-stakes decisions about people, the ability to explain may be worth more than a small gain in accuracy.
:::

## Common explanation methods

### Feature importance (global)

**Feature importance** shows which features matter most to the model **overall**, across all decisions. For example: "Repayment history is the strongest factor, then monthly sales, then loan size; age has almost no effect."

This helps engineers and managers check that the model relies on **sensible** factors. If an unexpected feature, such as the day of the week the application was submitted, ranks highly, something may be wrong.

### Local explanations: why this one decision?

**Local explanations** describe why the model made **one particular** decision. Two popular tools are **LIME** and **SHAP**. They estimate how much each feature pushed **this** prediction up or down compared with a typical case.

:::example Worked example: reading an additive explanation
A loan model gives applicant Mukamana an approval probability of **60%**. The bank's threshold is 50%, so she is approved. A SHAP-style explanation (numbers illustrative) shows how her features moved the probability from the **average applicant's 50%**:

| Feature (her value) | Effect on approval probability |
|---|---|
| Average applicant (starting point) | 50% |
| Repayment history: no late payments | **+18** points |
| Monthly sales: 180,000 RWF | **+7** points |
| Years in cooperative: 1 | **−4** points |
| Loan size requested: 900,000 RWF (large) | **−11** points |
| **Final probability** | **50 + 18 + 7 − 4 − 11 = 60%** |

Reading it: her excellent repayment history helped most; her large requested loan hurt most. The contributions add up exactly to the final prediction, which makes this kind of explanation easy to present to a loan officer or to the applicant.
:::

### Counterfactual explanations

A **counterfactual explanation** answers: **"What would need to change for a different result?"**

> "If your requested loan were 600,000 RWF instead of 900,000 RWF, the application would be approved."
> "If your monthly sales were 30,000 RWF higher, the application would be approved."

Counterfactuals are often the **most useful** explanations for ordinary people, because they are actionable: they show a path to a different outcome. Good counterfactuals use changes the person can actually make (loan size, savings) rather than ones they cannot (age, place of birth).

### Saliency maps for images

For image models, a **saliency map** highlights the **pixels** the model focused on. A crop-disease model should focus on the **spots on the leaf**, not on the soil, the farmer's hand or the background.

## Explanations reveal hidden problems

In a well-known 2016 study, researchers deliberately trained an image classifier to tell **wolves** from **huskies** using photos where the wolves were almost always on **snow**. The classifier seemed accurate. An explanation method revealed that it was mostly looking at the **snow in the background**, not at the animal. On a husky photographed in snow, it would say "wolf".

The model was **right for the wrong reasons**. Without the explanation, the team might have trusted it. This kind of hidden **shortcut** is common: medical-image models have been found to use hospital labels or ruler marks in X-rays instead of the medical signs.

:::example Worked example: debugging with a saliency map
A cassava-disease app works well in testing but fails in the field. Saliency maps show it focuses on **dark soil** around the leaves. Investigation reveals why: in the training data, most diseased leaves were photographed on dark research-farm soil, and most healthy leaves on lighter soil. The model learned "dark soil = disease". **Fix:** collect photos of both healthy and diseased leaves on many backgrounds, and crop images to the leaf before training.
:::

## Limits of explanations

Explanation tools are powerful, but they have limits:

- They give **approximations**, not the full truth of a complex model. LIME, for example, builds a simple model around one prediction to imitate the black box locally.
- **Different tools can disagree** about the same decision.
- An explanation can be **convincing and still misleading**: a neat list of reasons may hide that the model relies on a proxy.
- Explanations describe the **model**, not the **real world**. "Mobile-money activity raised the score" means the model uses it; it does not prove that mobile-money activity **causes** repayment.

So explanations **support** human judgement; they do not **replace** it.

:::mistake Common mistake
Treating an explanation as proof that a decision is fair. An explanation tells you **what** the model used. Whether that is acceptable is a separate question you must still ask, using the fairness checks from Chapter 19.
:::

:::rwanda In Rwanda
Rwanda's data-protection law (Chapter 21) gives people rights over decisions made about them using their personal data, and good practice everywhere is to explain important automated decisions in plain language. A practical standard for any organisation here: if your AI system affects someone's money, job, health or education, you should be able to tell them **the main reasons** and **what they could change**, in Kinyarwanda, English or French, and how to reach a person.
:::

## Summary

- **Black-box** models can be accurate but hard to understand; interpretable models can be read directly.
- Explanations matter for **affected people, organisations, engineers and regulators**, and help reveal hidden bias.
- Prefer the **simplest model that works**; otherwise explain complex models after training.
- Methods: **feature importance** (overall), **local explanations** such as LIME and SHAP (one decision), **counterfactuals** (what would change the result), **saliency maps** (images).
- Explanations catch models that are **right for the wrong reasons**, but they are **approximations** that support, not replace, human judgement.

## Key terms

- **Black box** — a model whose reasons for a decision are hard to see or explain.
- **Interpretable model** — a model whose logic can be read directly, such as a small decision tree.
- **Explainable AI (XAI)** — methods for making AI decisions understandable to people.
- **Feature importance** — how much each feature matters to a model overall.
- **Local explanation** — an explanation of one particular prediction.
- **LIME** — a method that explains one prediction by fitting a simple model around it.
- **SHAP** — a method that splits one prediction into contributions from each feature.
- **Counterfactual explanation** — a statement of what would need to change for a different outcome.
- **Saliency map** — an image highlighting the pixels an image model focused on.
- **Shortcut learning** — a model relying on an accidental pattern (like snow) instead of the real signal.

## Think deeper

1. Write a plain-language explanation, including a counterfactual, for a student whose scholarship application was rejected by an AI-supported system.
:::answer
"Thank you for applying. Your application was not selected this year. The main reasons were that your average mark (68%) was below most selected applicants, and one required recommendation letter was missing. If your average had been 72% or higher and all documents had been complete, your application would very likely have been selected. A member of our team reviewed this decision. If you believe something is wrong, you can ask for a review by replying to this message within 30 days."
:::
2. A model's feature-importance chart shows that "applicant's phone brand" is the third most important feature for loan approval. What should the team investigate?
:::answer
Phone brand probably acts as a proxy for income, and possibly for location or age. The team should check whether it reflects a legitimate, job-relevant signal or unfairly penalises poorer applicants who may still repay well. They should test performance and approval rates by income and region, try the model without the feature, and decide whether its use is acceptable, documenting the decision.
:::
3. Why can explanations from LIME and SHAP disagree about the same decision?
:::answer
Both are approximations built in different ways. LIME fits a simple model to the black box's behaviour on slightly changed versions of the input near this case; SHAP divides the prediction among features using a different mathematical approach and a chosen reference point. When features are related to each other, or the model behaves in complex ways, the two methods can assign credit differently. That is why explanations should be read as helpful estimates, not exact truth.
:::
